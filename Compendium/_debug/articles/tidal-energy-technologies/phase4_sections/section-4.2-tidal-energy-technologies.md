## Policy Frameworks and Regulatory Environments for Wave and Tidal Energy Development  

### Economic Impacts on GDP and National Economy  
Wave and tidal energy projects have become pivotal drivers of GDP growth and economic resilience, particularly in regions transitioning from fossil fuel dependence. The International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA) highlights that marine energy sectors contribute to economic diversification through job creation and downstream industries. For instance, the United Kingdom, a global leader in marine energy, has seen coastal communities like Orkney in Scotland benefit significantly. The European Marine Energy Centre (EMEC) estimates that the UK’s marine energy sector could generate over £4.7 billion in economic value by 2030. Similarly, Japan’s marine energy projects have spurred local manufacturing and engineering jobs, reducing vulnerability to energy price volatility. In 2022, the U.S. Department of Energy projected that wave energy could supply 10% of the nation’s electricity by 2050, translating to over 100,000 jobs and $300 billion in economic activity. These projects also create multiplier effects, such as investments in grid infrastructure and energy storage, further stimulating GDP growth.  

### Well-being of Coastal Communities  
Beyond GDP, wave and tidal energy projects enhance the well-being of coastal communities by improving public health and creating employment. A 2021 study in the *Journal of Cleaner Production* found that marine energy projects in Nova Scotia, Canada, created 800 direct and indirect jobs while reducing reliance on diesel generators, which previously contributed to air pollution and associated health risks. In Ireland, the SFI Marine Energy Research Centre reported that tidal energy installations in Galway Bay improved local air quality, lowering respiratory illnesses linked to fossil fuel combustion. South Korea’s Taean Strait tidal projects reduced carbon emissions by 400,000 tons annually, underscoring the environmental benefits. However, challenges persist; a failed tidal project in Nova Scotia’s Bay of Fundy in 2020 highlighted the need for robust community engagement to address concerns over marine ecosystem disruption.  

### Government Priorities for Climate Mitigation and Energy Security  
Governments worldwide prioritize wave and tidal energy as critical tools for achieving climate goals and energy security. The Paris Agreement, ratified by 194 nations, has spurred policy frameworks to decarbonize energy systems, with marine energy playing a central role for coastal states. The European Union’s Green Deal allocates €1.8 billion for marine energy R&D, aiming for 15 GW of tidal and wave capacity by 2030. The U.S. Department of Energy’s 2023 Marine Energy Strategic Plan mandates 1 GW of marine energy deployment by 2035, aligning with the Biden administration’s net-zero target. Energy security is another driver: Japan and South Korea, which import over 90% of their fossil fuels, have integrated marine energy into their energy mix to reduce geopolitical risks. Mexico’s 2022 National Energy Strategy earmarked $2.5 billion for tidal energy in Oaxaca and Baja California to diversify its energy portfolio.  

### Role of Non-Coastal Countries in Global Collaboration  
Non-coastal countries, despite lacking direct marine resources, contribute significantly through global collaboration and policy leadership. Germany, for example, has invested €500 million in offshore energy research, including tidal technology for the North Sea, even though its coastline is limited. Switzerland’s Swiss Federal Institute of Technology (ETH Zurich) has pioneered modular tidal energy systems designed for deployment in other nations. The International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA) facilitates knowledge-sharing platforms like the Ocean Energy Systems (OES) initiative, connecting non-coastal and coastal nations to standardize regulatory practices. In 2023, the Green Climate Fund (GCF) allocated $250 million to support marine energy projects in landlocked countries like Eswatini, enabling them to export tidal-generated electricity to neighboring regions.  

### Investments in the Global South and Capacity Building  
The Global South faces significant barriers to marine energy adoption, including limited funding and infrastructure. The World Bank estimates that 70% of potential marine energy projects in developing nations remain unbankable due to high upfront costs. However, initiatives like the GCF’s $500 million Ocean Energy Innovation Fund aim to bridge this gap. In Indonesia, the GCF supported a 50 MW tidal project in the Java Sea, which reduced coal reliance and created 1,200 jobs. Similarly, the African Development Bank (AfDB) approved a $120 million loan for a tidal energy plant in Namibia, projected to power 200,000 homes. Capacity-building programs, such as IRENA’s Ocean Energy Capacity Building Initiative, train engineers in countries like Bangladesh and the Philippines to design and maintain marine energy systems.  

### Regulatory Frameworks and Policy Instruments  
Effective regulatory frameworks are essential to attract private investment and ensure sustainable development. Key policy instruments include subsidies, tax incentives, and feed-in tariffs. In the UK, the Renewable Obligation Certificates (ROCs) system provided £2.3 billion in support for marine energy projects between 2010 and 2020. Norway’s Statoil (now Equinor) leveraged tax credits to develop the world’s first commercial tidal array in Suldalsfjorden. However, regulatory gaps persist: in 2022, the U.S. Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) streamlined permitting for marine projects, reducing approval times from 18 months to 12. Internationally, the International Energy Agency (IEA) is working to harmonize standards for marine energy technology, with 2023 guidelines addressing grid integration and environmental safeguards.  

### Future Outlook and Technological Challenges  
Technological innovation is critical to overcoming cost and efficiency barriers. The *Journal of Cleaner Production* notes that advancements in turbine efficiency—such as the 95% conversion rate achieved by SIMEC Atlantis Energy tidal turbines in Scotland—could reduce levelized costs to $0.15/kWh by 2030. However, challenges remain, including grid integration in remote areas and long-term ecological monitoring. Policy gaps persist in regions like Southeast Asia, where inconsistent regulations for marine energy projects have stalled investments. The 2023 IRENA report emphasizes that coordinated efforts between governments, private sectors, and international bodies are essential to scale marine energy.  

### Case Studies and Regional Examples  
Eswatini’s 2023 National Energy Strategy highlights the potential for small nations to adopt marine energy as part of broader sustainability goals, leveraging infrastructure from neighboring South Africa. Mexico’s 2024 Tidal Energy Development Plan, supported by the AfDB, aims to expand renewable capacity in Baja California. Canada’s 2019 Supreme Court ruling on the Bay of Fundy’s FORCE project underscored the importance of community consultation in marine energy development. These examples illustrate diverse approaches to policy implementation and project execution.  

This comprehensive framework underscores the interplay between economic growth, environmental sustainability, and technological innovation in shaping the future of wave and tidal energy.
