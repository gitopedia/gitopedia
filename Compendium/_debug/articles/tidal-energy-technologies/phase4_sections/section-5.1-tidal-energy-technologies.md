### Global Projects

## Economic and Social Impact of Wave and Tidal Energy Projects  

### Economic Diversification and Job Creation  
Wave and tidal energy projects have emerged as catalysts for economic diversification, particularly in regions historically dependent on fossil fuels or agriculture. In the United Kingdom, the Crown Estate’s marine energy licensing framework has spurred over £1 billion in investments in tidal and wave projects since 2010, directly creating more than 1,000 jobs in engineering and manufacturing. Scotland, which once relied heavily on oil and gas, has leveraged tidal energy to diversify its economy. The European Marine Energy Centre (EMEC) in Orkney, a global hub for marine energy testing, attracts over 50 companies and generates an estimated £200 million annually for the local economy. This shift has insulated the region from fossil fuel market volatility, as demonstrated by Portugal’s Aguçadoura Wave Farm, which, during its operational phase, reduced energy import bills by 20%—a critical factor for small island nations like the Maldives or Tuvalu, which spend up to 30% of GDP on imported fuels.  

Japan’s New Energy and Industrial Technology Development Organization (NEDO)-led Marine Renewable Energy Research Center has similarly catalyzed economic growth in coastal prefectures like Kagoshima, where tidal projects have boosted regional GDP by 1.5%. The *Journal of Cleaner Production* estimates that marine energy could create 20,000 direct and indirect jobs along the U.S. Atlantic coast by 2030, particularly in maintenance, grid integration, and environmental monitoring. For example, Nova Scotia’s Sable Island Tidal Energy Project employs local fishermen in turbine monitoring, blending traditional livelihoods with renewable energy expertise. Such hybrid models not only preserve cultural practices but also ensure a skilled workforce for emerging technologies.  

### Environmental Benefits and Health Impacts  
By displacing fossil fuels, marine energy projects significantly reduce greenhouse gas emissions and air pollution-related health risks. South Korea’s Busan Tidal Power Plant, operational since 2011, offsets 150,000 tons of CO₂ annually, improving air quality in a densely populated region. The European Commission’s 2022 report highlights that tidal barrages, when designed with fish-friendly turbines, can coexist with marine ecosystems, unlike coal-fired plants that degrade water quality and harm biodiversity. For instance, Nova Scotia’s Sable Island project incorporates advanced turbine designs to minimize fish mortality, ensuring ecological balance while generating 1 MW of power.  

In contrast, coal-fired power plants emit particulate matter and mercury, contributing to respiratory diseases and neurological disorders. Marine energy systems, however, typically have minimal ecological footprints. The UK’s Climate Change Act mandates a 78% emissions reduction by 2035, with marine energy projected to supply 10% of the nation’s electricity by 2040. Similarly, Norway’s Equinor integrates tidal energy into its offshore wind portfolio, aiming to reduce emissions from oil and gas operations by 40% by 2030.  

### Government Priorities and Energy Security  
Governments worldwide prioritize marine energy to meet climate mitigation targets under the Paris Agreement. The UK’s Climate Change Act, which mandates a 78% emissions reduction by 2035, positions marine energy as a cornerstone for achieving this goal, with projections of 10% electricity supply from wave and tidal sources by 2040. Australia’s Wave Energy Research Centre has developed the CETO system, generating 1 MW for Fremantle and reducing reliance on gas imports. In Canada, the Federal Energy Strategy identifies the Bay of Fundy’s tidal projects as a potential source for 10% of the country’s electricity needs by 2030.  

Energy security is another critical driver. Coastal nations like Australia and Canada, which rely on imported energy, are investing in marine energy to insulate themselves from geopolitical risks. The *Journal of Cleaner Production* notes that Australia’s CETO system has demonstrated the feasibility of integrating marine energy into existing grids, reducing fossil fuel dependence. Mexico’s National Commission for Energy Control (CENACE) has approved a $2 billion project in Baja California, combining tidal energy with offshore wind to supply 8% of the region’s power by 2028 while creating 3,000 jobs in coastal communities.  

### Role of Non-Coastal Countries in Research and Policy  
Non-coastal countries like Germany and Switzerland play pivotal roles in advancing marine energy through research and international collaboration. Germany’s Fraunhofer Institute leads the MARINET2 project, a €12.5 million EU-funded initiative that provides access to specialized testing facilities for tidal and wave technologies. Switzerland’s ETH Zurich has pioneered hybrid systems integrating marine energy with hydrogen production, offering a blueprint for decentralized energy networks.  

These nations also influence global policy frameworks. Germany’s advocacy for the EU’s Renewable Energy Directive (RED II) has incentivized cross-border collaboration, while Switzerland’s participation in the International Energy Agency’s Ocean Energy Systems (IEA OES) promotes standardization and cost reduction. Such efforts ensure that even landlocked countries can contribute to the global marine energy transition by sharing technological expertise and funding mechanisms.  

### Investments in the Global South and Challenges  
The Global South faces unique challenges in marine energy adoption due to funding constraints and infrastructure gaps. The World Bank estimates that developing nations require $1.2 trillion annually to meet clean energy targets, with marine energy receiving less than 1% of this allocation. However, initiatives like the Green Climate Fund (GCF) have supported projects in India and Kenya. For example, India’s GCF-funded 5 MW tidal project in the Andaman Sea aims to provide electricity to remote island communities, while Kenya’s Lake Turkana Wind Power has integrated tidal energy into its hybrid grid, reducing diesel dependency by 30%.  

Capacity-building programs, such as IRENA’s Marine Energy Academy, are critical for training technicians in Bangladesh and Vietnam to maintain tidal infrastructure. Despite these efforts, regulatory hurdles and limited private-sector engagement remain barriers. The UK’s Ocean Energy Cluster, which reduced commercialization timelines by 50% through public-private partnerships, offers a replicable model for developing nations.  

### Regulatory Frameworks and Technological Innovations  
Regulatory frameworks are essential for scaling marine energy. In the U.S., the Bureau of Ocean Energy Management (BOEM) offers 25-year leases for offshore energy projects, attracting private investment. France’s feed-in tariff (FiT) of €180/MWh for marine energy has spurred projects like the Paimpol-Bréhat tidal farm, which generates 5 MW for 2,000 homes. The EU’s Marine Strategy Framework Directive (MSFD) mandates environmental impact assessments (EIAs), ensuring projects like Canada’s Strait of Canso tidal farm—which employs co-management agreements with the Mi’kmaq First Nation—balance ecological and community interests.  

Technological advancements are driving cost reductions. The International Energy Agency (IEA) projects marine energy costs could drop from $250/MWh to $100/MWh by 2030, driven by innovations like Ocean Energy’s O2 tidal turbine, which uses self-adjusting blades to optimize energy capture. Modular designs, such as the Ocean Renewable Power Company’s PowerBuoy, enable scalable deployment in diverse coastal environments.  

### Case Studies: Eswatini and Mexico  
Eswatini’s hybrid marine-solar project, supported by the African Development Bank, aims to generate 10% of the country’s electricity from tidal sources by 2030, reducing diesel imports by 40%. Meanwhile, Mexico’s Baja California project, a $2 billion public-private partnership, combines tidal energy with offshore wind to supply 8% of the region’s power by 2028. Both cases underscore the potential of marine energy to address energy poverty and climate resilience in developing economies.  

These examples illustrate the transformative potential of wave and tidal energy in reshaping global energy systems, fostering economic resilience, and advancing climate action.
