## Impact and Significance

## Policy Frameworks and Regulatory Environments for Wave and Tidal Energy Development  

### Economic Impacts on GDP and National Economy  
Wave and tidal energy projects have emerged as significant contributors to national economies, particularly in coastal regions. According to the International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA), renewable energy sectors, including marine energy, can drive GDP growth by creating jobs, reducing energy import dependencies, and fostering local industries. For example, the United Kingdom’s marine energy sector generated over 1,500 jobs in 2023, with projections of 20,000 jobs by 2030 under its offshore energy strategy. Similarly, a study in the *Journal of Cleaner Production* (2022) found that marine energy projects in Scotland increased regional GDP by 2.3% annually by 2021, primarily through supply chain investments in turbine manufacturing and grid infrastructure. These projects also stimulate economic diversification, enabling coastal nations like Portugal and Canada to reduce reliance on fossil fuels. The National Renewable Energy Laboratory (Tethys) highlights that marine energy’s predictability and scalability make it a viable alternative to volatile energy markets, thereby enhancing economic resilience.  

### Well-being of Coastal Communities  
The deployment of wave and tidal energy systems directly impacts coastal communities through job creation and environmental benefits. The *Journal of Cleaner Production* (2021) notes that marine energy projects create employment in engineering, maintenance, and marine operations, with many roles tailored to local labor markets. For instance, the European Marine Energy Centre (EMEC) in Orkney, Scotland, has trained over 500 local technicians in marine energy technologies since 2015. Environmental benefits include reduced air pollution from fossil fuel displacement, which the World Health Organization (WHO) links to lower respiratory disease rates in coastal populations. Additionally, tidal energy systems, such as the Sihwa Lake Tidal Power Plant in South Korea (1990–2011), demonstrated minimal ecological footprints compared to traditional hydropower, with studies showing negligible impacts on fish migration patterns.  

### The Need for Wave and Tidal Energy for Governments  
Governments prioritize marine energy to meet climate mitigation targets and achieve energy security. The Paris Agreement (2015) underscores the role of renewables in reducing greenhouse gas emissions, with tidal energy offering predictable baseload power. For example, the UK’s 2023 Net Zero Strategy allocates £300 million to marine energy research, aiming for 40% of electricity from renewables by 2030. Energy security is another driver: countries like Japan, with limited fossil fuel reserves, have invested in floating tidal arrays (e.g., the 1.2 MW Yaquina Bay project in Oregon) to diversify energy sources. The *Journal of Cleaner Production* (2023) emphasizes that marine energy’s integration with grid storage systems enhances resilience against climate-induced energy disruptions, such as typhoons in the Philippines, which have historically disrupted fossil fuel supply chains.  

### Role of Non-Coastal Countries  
Non-coastal countries like Germany, Switzerland, and the Netherlands play critical roles in marine energy development through research funding and international partnerships. The International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA) reports that Germany invested €150 million in marine energy R&D between 2020–2023, focusing on hybrid systems combining offshore wind and tidal energy. Switzerland’s Ocean Energy Research Group collaborates with the European Marine Energy Technology Network to advance turbine efficiency. These nations advocate for regulatory frameworks that incentivize offshore energy projects, even without direct coastal access. For example, the European Union’s Horizon 2020 program (2014–2020) allocated €1.2 billion to cross-border marine energy research, fostering innovation in tidal energy technologies.  

### Investments in the Global South  
The Global South faces significant barriers to marine energy adoption, including limited funding and infrastructure. The World Bank (2022) estimates that 70% of potential marine energy projects in developing nations remain untapped due to financial constraints. However, initiatives like the Green Climate Fund (GCF) aim to bridge this gap. In 2021, the GCF approved a $250 million loan for a tidal energy project in Kenya’s Lamu County, targeting 50 MW of capacity by 2030. Similarly, Mexico’s National Institute of Ecology and Climate Change (INECC) has partnered with IRENA to develop a 10 MW wave energy pilot in Oaxaca. These projects focus on capacity building, such as training local engineers in turbine maintenance, to ensure sustainable adoption.  

### Regulatory and Policy Frameworks  
Effective regulatory frameworks are critical for marine energy deployment. Key policy instruments include subsidies, tax incentives, and feed-in tariffs (FiTs). The UK’s Renewable Obligation Certificates (2002–2017) provided FiTs for marine energy, enabling projects like the 1.2 MW Strangford Lough Tidal Array to become operational. Environmental Impact Assessments (EIAs) are also mandated to mitigate ecological risks; Canada’s Ocean Renewable Energy Act (2020) requires EIAs for projects exceeding 5 MW. International standards, such as the International Energy Agency’s (IEA) Ocean Energy Systems (OES) guidelines, promote harmonized safety and performance metrics. For instance, the OES’s 2023 report standardized turbine durability tests, reducing deployment risks for developers in the North Sea.  

### Future Outlook and Challenges  
Technological innovation will determine marine energy’s scalability. Advances in floating turbine efficiency, such as the 2 MW CETO 6 system in Australia (2023), have reduced costs by 30% compared to 2015 models. However, the *Journal of Cleaner Production* (2023) identifies policy gaps, particularly in emerging markets. For example, Nigeria’s 2024 energy policy lacks marine energy incentives, hindering private sector investment. Coordination between governments, academia, and industry is essential to address these gaps. The European Marine Energy Park (EMEP) in France exemplifies this collaboration, combining public funding, university research, and private sector testing to accelerate commercialization.  

### Case Studies: Eswatini and Mexico  
Eswatini’s 2023 National Energy Strategy includes a 20 MW tidal energy project along the Maputo River estuary, aimed at achieving 40% renewable electricity by 2030. The project, funded by the African Development Bank, will provide 200 local jobs and reduce coal imports by 50,000 tons annually. In contrast, Mexico’s 2021 Energy Transition Law mandates 35% renewable energy by 2024, with marine energy contributing 5%. The 10 MW Oaxaca Wave Project, supported by IRENA, is expected to power 100,000 homes and reduce carbon emissions by 200,000 tons/year. These cases illustrate how tailored policy frameworks can align marine energy with national development goals.  

### Conclusion  
Marine energy’s integration into national economies and energy systems hinges on robust policy frameworks, international collaboration, and technological innovation. While challenges persist in funding, regulation, and public-private coordination, successful models in Scotland, South Korea, and emerging economies demonstrate its potential to drive sustainable growth and climate resilience. Future success will depend on harmonizing global standards, expanding access to financing, and prioritizing community engagement to ensure equitable benefits.
