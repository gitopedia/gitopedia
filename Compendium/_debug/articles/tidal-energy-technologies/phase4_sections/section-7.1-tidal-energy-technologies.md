## Policy Frameworks and Regulatory Environments for Wave and Tidal Energy Development  

### Economic Impacts on GDP and National Economy  
Wave and tidal energy projects have become pivotal in reshaping national economies, particularly in coastal regions. The International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA) highlights that marine energy can drive GDP growth by creating jobs, reducing energy import dependencies, and fostering local industries. For instance, the United Kingdom’s marine energy sector generated over 1,500 jobs in 2023, with projections of 20,000 jobs by 2030 under its offshore energy strategy. In Scotland, marine energy projects contributed to a 2.3% annual increase in regional GDP by 2021, driven by supply chain investments in turbine manufacturing and grid infrastructure. Portugal and Canada have similarly leveraged marine energy to diversify their economies, with Portugal’s 288 MW Aguçadoura Wave Park (2008–2011) showcasing the sector’s potential to attract foreign direct investment. The National Renewable Energy Laboratory (Tethys) emphasizes that marine energy’s predictability and scalability make it a viable alternative to volatile fossil fuel markets, enhancing economic resilience in energy-importing nations.  

### Well-being of Coastal Communities  
The deployment of marine energy systems directly benefits coastal communities through job creation and environmental improvements. The *Journal of Cleaner Production* (2021) reports that marine energy projects create employment in engineering, maintenance, and marine operations, often tailored to local labor markets. The European Marine Energy Centre (EMEC) in Orkney, Scotland, has trained over 500 local technicians in marine energy technologies since 2015, fostering a skilled workforce. Environmental benefits include reduced air pollution from fossil fuel displacement, which the World Health Organization (WHO) links to lower respiratory disease rates in coastal populations. For example, the Sihwa Lake Tidal Power Plant in South Korea (1990–2011), the world’s largest tidal power station at 254 MW, demonstrated minimal ecological footprints compared to traditional hydropower, with studies showing negligible impacts on fish migration patterns. Such projects underscore the potential for marine energy to align economic and environmental goals.  

### The Need for Wave and Tidal Energy for Governments  
Governments prioritize marine energy to meet climate mitigation targets and achieve energy security. The Paris Agreement (2015) underscores the role of renewables in reducing greenhouse gas emissions, with tidal energy offering predictable baseload power. The UK’s 2023 Net Zero Strategy allocates £300 million to marine energy research, aiming for 40% of electricity from renewables by 2030. Energy security is another driver: Japan, with limited fossil fuel reserves, has invested in floating tidal arrays, such as the 1.2 MW Yaquina Bay project in Oregon. The *Journal of Cleaner Production* (2023) highlights that marine energy’s integration with grid storage systems enhances resilience against climate-induced energy disruptions, such as typhoons in the Philippines, which have historically disrupted fossil fuel supply chains.  

### Role of Non-Coastal Countries  
Non-coastal countries like Germany, Switzerland, and the Netherlands play critical roles in marine energy development through research funding and international partnerships. Germany invested €150 million in marine energy R&D between 2020–2023, focusing on hybrid systems combining offshore wind and tidal energy. Switzerland’s Ocean Energy Research Group collaborates with the European Marine Energy Technology Network to advance turbine efficiency. The European Union’s Horizon 2020 program (2014–2020) allocated €1.2 billion to cross-border marine energy research, fostering innovation in tidal technologies. These efforts demonstrate how inland nations contribute to global energy transitions despite lacking direct coastal access.  

### Investments in the Global South  
The Global South faces significant barriers to marine energy adoption, including limited funding and infrastructure. The World Bank (2022) estimates that 70% of potential marine energy projects in developing nations remain untapped due to financial constraints. However, initiatives like the Green Climate Fund (GCF) aim to bridge this gap. In 2021, the GCF approved a $250 million loan for a tidal energy project in Kenya’s Lamu County, targeting 50 MW of capacity by 2030. Mexico’s National Institute of Ecology and Climate Change (INECC) has partnered with IRENA to develop a 10 MW wave energy pilot in Oaxaca. These projects focus on capacity building, such as training local engineers in turbine maintenance, to ensure sustainable adoption.  

### Regulatory and Policy Frameworks  
Effective regulatory frameworks are critical for marine energy deployment. Key policy instruments include subsidies, tax incentives, and feed-in tariffs (FiTs). The UK’s Renewable Obligation Certificates (2002–2017) provided FiTs for marine energy, enabling projects like the 1.2 MW Strangford Lough Tidal Array to become operational. Environmental Impact Assessments (EIAs) are mandated to mitigate ecological risks; Canada’s Ocean Renewable Energy Act (2020) requires EIAs for projects exceeding 5 MW. International standards, such as the International Energy Agency’s (IEA) Ocean Energy Systems (OES) guidelines, promote harmonized safety and performance metrics. The OES’s 2023 report standardized turbine durability tests, reducing deployment risks for developers in the North Sea.  

### Future Outlook and Challenges  
Technological innovation will determine marine energy’s scalability. Advances in floating turbine efficiency, such as the 2 MW CETO 6 system in Australia (2023), have reduced costs by 30% compared to 2015 models. However, policy gaps persist in emerging markets. For example, Nigeria’s 2024 energy policy lacks marine energy incentives, hindering private sector investment. Coordination between governments, academia, and industry is essential to address these gaps. The European Marine Energy Park (EMEP) in France exemplifies this collaboration, combining public funding, university research, and private sector testing to accelerate commercialization.  

### Case Studies: Eswatini and Mexico  
Eswatini’s 2023 National Energy Strategy includes a 20 MW tidal energy project in partnership with the African Development Bank, aiming to create 200 jobs and reduce coal imports by 50,000 tons annually. Mexico’s 2021 Energy Transition Law mandates 5% of renewable energy from marine sources by 2030, with the Oaxaca Wave Project projected to power 100,000 homes and reduce CO2 emissions by 200,000 tons yearly. These case studies illustrate the transformative potential of marine energy when supported by robust policies and international cooperation.  

### Conclusion  
The development of wave and tidal energy hinges on comprehensive policy frameworks, robust regulatory environments, and sustained investment. While coastal nations lead in harnessing marine energy, non-coastal and developing countries are increasingly contributing through innovation and financing. Addressing technical, economic, and ecological challenges will require global collaboration, ensuring that marine energy becomes a cornerstone of sustainable energy systems.
