## Geopolitics of Nuclear Fusion

Nuclear fusion, the process of merging atomic nuclei to release energy, has emerged as a transformative force in global energy and security strategies. Unlike fission-based nuclear energy, fusion is often heralded as a safer, cleaner, and nearly inexhaustible energy source, with the potential to reshape energy markets, reduce fossil fuel dependence, and address climate change. However, its development is deeply intertwined with geopolitical dynamics, ranging from international collaborations to resource competition and institutional policy frameworks. This section examines the evolving geopolitical landscape of nuclear fusion, focusing on key players, strategic initiatives, and the broader implications for global energy security.

### International Collaborations and Competitions

Nuclear fusion has become a focal point for multilateral diplomacy, with major projects serving as both unifying and contentious platforms. The **International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor (ITER)**, launched in 1985 and under construction since 2007 in southern France, epitomizes this trend. Funded by 35 nations—including the United States, China, India, Russia, and members of the European Union—ITER aims to demonstrate the feasibility of sustained fusion reactions. Initially projected to cost €5 billion and operational by 2016, the project has faced significant delays and cost overruns, with the total budget now exceeding €25 billion. Despite these challenges, ITER remains a critical experiment in global energy cooperation, though its progress has been shadowed by geopolitical tensions. Notably, Russia suspended its contributions to the project in 2022 amid Western sanctions over the Ukraine war, underscoring how fusion diplomacy is inextricably linked to broader international relations.

In contrast to multilateral efforts, nations are also pursuing unilateral advancements in fusion technology. The **United States** has prioritized fusion through the Department of Energy’s $500 million investment in private-sector initiatives like General Atomics and Helion Energy, aiming for commercial deployment by the 2030s. **China** has made significant strides with its Experimental Advanced Superconducting Tokamak (EAST) reactor, achieving record-breaking plasma temperatures (up to 120 million degrees Celsius) and durations (over 1,000 seconds), positioning itself as a global leader in fusion research. The **European Union**, through Horizon Europe, has allocated €10 billion to fusion projects, emphasizing energy independence amid the fallout from the Russian gas crisis. Meanwhile, **private firms** such as UK-based Tokamak Energy and Canada’s CTFusion are accelerating innovation, raising questions about how publicly and privately developed fusion technologies might intersect in the future. For example, Helion Energy announced in 2024 that its fusion reactor could achieve net energy gain by 2025, potentially disrupting the energy market.

### Resource Geopolitics and Strategic Competition

The geopolitics of fusion extends beyond reactors to the critical resources required for fuel, infrastructure, and technology. **Lithium**, essential for producing tritium—a fusion fuel—is a key focal point. Australia, Chile, and Argentina control over 70% of global lithium reserves, creating a potential bottleneck for fusion development. Similarly, **helium-3**, a rare isotope ideal for advanced fusion reactions, is predominantly found in lunar regolith, prompting speculative discussions about space mining and its geopolitical ramifications. While current fusion projects rely on deuterium (extracted from seawater) and lithium-based tritium breeding, the long-term viability of these fuels hinges on securing stable supply chains, which could exacerbate resource competition.

**Rare earth elements** (REEs), crucial for superconducting magnets in tokamaks and stellarators, further complicate the landscape. China dominates 90% of global rare earth processing, giving it significant leverage over fusion technology development. The **European Institute for International Law and International Relations (EIIR)** has highlighted how resource dependencies could replicate existing fossil fuel dynamics, with nations forming strategic alliances or rivalries over access to critical materials. For instance, the EU’s Green Deal emphasizes decoupling from Chinese rare earth processing, a move that could indirectly influence fusion energy strategies. In 2023, the EU launched the Critical Raw Materials Act to secure domestic supply chains, including for fusion-related materials.

### Institutional Contributions and Policy Frameworks

The **EIIR** plays a pivotal role in analyzing fusion’s geopolitical implications, integrating legal, strategic, and policy perspectives. Its **Solidarity4All project (2024–2026)**, funded by the European Commission, explores how fusion energy could strengthen EU solidarity through shared infrastructure and knowledge transfer, particularly in addressing energy poverty in marginalized regions. The institute’s research also examines fusion’s role in **EU enlargement strategies**, assessing how energy cooperation might facilitate Ukraine’s integration into European energy networks, thereby countering Russian influence. For example, the EIIR proposed a fusion-based energy corridor connecting Ukraine to EU grid systems, reducing reliance on Russian energy exports.

The EIIR has also scrutinized **China’s growing influence in Africa**, questioning whether Beijing’s investments in infrastructure and resources—such as lithium extraction in the Democratic Republic of Congo—constitute a new form of colonialism or a model of mutual benefit. This analysis is critical for understanding how fusion’s resource demands could reshape global power dynamics. Additionally, the institute’s 2022 symposium on the **end of the Russian-Ukrainian war** underscored fusion’s potential as a neutralizing force in energy geopolitics, proposing that post-war energy collaborations could foster regional stability. The symposium highlighted a hypothetical scenario where fusion technology was deployed in a shared facility in Eastern Europe, funded jointly by EU and Russian entities to rebuild trust.

### Future Implications and Geopolitical Scenarios

As fusion technology advances, its geopolitical impact will depend on how nations manage its deployment. A **collaborative scenario** could see fusion as a tool for global energy equity, with international bodies like the **International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA)** regulating technology sharing to prevent monopolization. Conversely, a **competitive scenario** might lead to a "fusion arms race," where nations hoard resources, restrict intellectual property, and weaponize energy security. The **European Green Deal’s emphasis on fusion** as part of a decarbonized energy mix could serve as a model for multilateral governance, though success hinges on overcoming technical and political hurdles. For instance, the EU’s 2030 target for fusion to contribute 10% of its energy mix depends on resolving issues like tritium breeding and waste management.

The EIIR’s research also warns of **regional disparities** in fusion adoption. Wealthier nations may achieve commercial fusion first, exacerbating energy inequalities, while developing countries could face exclusion from the technology. This dynamic mirrors historical patterns in renewable energy access, such as the uneven distribution of solar panel manufacturing. Furthermore, fusion’s potential to reduce fossil fuel dependence might destabilize oil-rich economies, prompting retaliatory policies. In 2024, Saudi Arabia announced a fusion research initiative to diversify its energy portfolio, signaling a shift in traditional energy powers.

### Conclusion

The geopolitics of nuclear fusion is a complex interplay of technological innovation, resource competition, and international diplomacy. While projects like ITER and national initiatives highlight the potential for collaboration, the strategic control of critical materials and the involvement of institutions like the EIIR underscore the risks of fragmentation and inequality. As fusion moves closer to commercialization, the global community faces a critical choice: to harness its transformative power through inclusive cooperation or to fragment along lines of resource control and geopolitical rivalry. The outcomes of these decisions will shape not only the energy landscape but also the broader trajectory of international relations in the 21st century.
