## Introduction

## Geopolitics of Nuclear Fusion

Nuclear fusion, often hailed as a potential cornerstone of a sustainable energy future, is deeply intertwined with geopolitical dynamics that span resource allocation, international collaboration, and ethical governance. The European Institute for International Law and International Relations (EIIR) has emerged as a pivotal actor in analyzing these dynamics, linking fusion energy to broader themes of global governance, energy transition, and strategic competition. This section explores the multifaceted geopolitical landscape of nuclear fusion, drawing on the EIIR’s research, technical challenges, and ethical frameworks to illuminate the complexities of this emerging field.

### Role of Nuclear Fusion in Energy Policy and International Collaboration

The EIIR’s publications highlight nuclear fusion’s potential to reshape global energy systems, particularly in the context of climate change and decarbonization. Fusion energy, which replicates the processes powering stars, offers a theoretically limitless supply of clean energy, free from the long-lived radioactive waste associated with nuclear fission. However, its geopolitical significance lies not only in its technical promise but also in its capacity to foster international cooperation. The EIIR emphasizes that fusion projects, such as the International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor (ITER), represent "sustainability satellites" within the broader energy transition, requiring unprecedented levels of multilateral engagement.

ITER, a 35-nation collaboration headquartered in France, exemplifies the geopolitical interdependence inherent in fusion development. Member states include the European Union, the United States, China, India, Japan, Russia, and South Korea—countries with divergent strategic priorities. The EIIR notes that such projects necessitate "adaptive governance models," balancing innovation with accountability while navigating power asymmetries. For instance, the EU’s leadership in funding and hosting ITER contrasts with China’s growing technological capabilities and Russia’s historical role in fusion research. These dynamics raise questions about representation, decision-making authority, and the obligations of non-member states, which may be affected by fusion advancements without participating directly.

### Technical and Ethical Challenges in Fusion Development

The technical hurdles of achieving commercial fusion are mirrored by ethical complexities that shape its geopolitical trajectory. One critical issue is tritium management. Tritium, a radioactive isotope of hydrogen, is essential as a fuel in most current fusion designs. However, its supply chain is heavily reliant on fission reactors, which complicates fusion’s independence from legacy nuclear infrastructure. The EIIR’s research underscores the "supply ethics" of this dependency, as well as safety and proliferation concerns. Tritium’s dual-use potential—its application in nuclear weapons—necessitates stringent security protocols, particularly in regions with geopolitical tensions.

Alternative fusion cycles, such as deuterium-deuterium (D-D) or deuterium-helium-3 (D-He3) reactions, are being explored to mitigate tritium reliance. Yet, these alternatives face significant technical barriers, including the scarcity of helium-3 and the difficulty of achieving the necessary plasma conditions. The EIIR argues that such challenges demand "responsible innovation," with ethical frameworks guiding decisions about resource allocation and risk management. For example, the EU’s Horizon Europe program has prioritized fusion research, but critics question whether this investment could be better directed toward renewable energy or energy efficiency.

### International Collaborations and Governance Models

The governance of fusion projects is a microcosm of broader geopolitical tensions. The EIIR’s analysis of ITER highlights the need for "dynamic decision-making" to address uncertainties in timelines and outcomes. While ITER aims to demonstrate the feasibility of fusion by the 2040s, delays and budget overruns have sparked debates about accountability. The EU, which contributes 45% of ITER’s funding, has faced criticism for its dominant role in shaping the project’s direction, raising concerns about equitable participation. Meanwhile, China’s increasing investment in fusion research, including its Experimental Advanced Superconducting Tokamak (EAST), signals a shift in global scientific leadership and underscores the competitive dimension of fusion development.

The EIIR also examines the implications of non-state actors in fusion governance. Private companies, such as General Fusion and Tokamak Energy, are accelerating progress through novel approaches like magnetized target fusion and high-field tokamaks. While these ventures democratize innovation, they also complicate traditional governance structures. For example, the U.S. Department of Energy’s recent investments in private fusion firms have raised questions about the balance between public and private interests. The EIIR advocates for "precautionary principles" in such partnerships, ensuring that commercial ambitions align with public safety and environmental stewardship.

### Resource Allocation and Equitable Access

Resource allocation is a central geopolitical issue in fusion energy. The EIIR’s Solidarity4All project, funded by the European Commission, explores how equitable access to energy technologies can mitigate regional disparities and enhance global solidarity. Fusion, as a high-cost, high-tech endeavor, risks exacerbating the energy divide between developed and developing nations. For instance, the EU’s focus on fusion may divert attention from decentralized renewable solutions that are more immediately accessible to low-income countries. The EIIR calls for policies that "shape resource allocation" through taxes, incentives, and international aid, ensuring that fusion benefits are not confined to a handful of technologically advanced nations.

This challenge is compounded by the dual-use nature of fusion research. The same materials and expertise required for peaceful energy production can be repurposed for nuclear weapons proliferation. The EIIR stresses the importance of non-proliferation frameworks, particularly in regions with nuclear ambitions, such as the Middle East and South Asia. For example, Iran’s nuclear program has historically drawn scrutiny for its potential military applications, and similar concerns could arise if fusion technology becomes a conduit for nuclear weaponization.

### Public Trust and Transparency

Building public trust is essential for the long-term success of fusion energy. The EIIR’s work on policy dialogue and public engagement underscores the need for transparency in fusion projects, especially those with transnational implications. For example, the EU’s Green Deal initiative includes fusion energy as a key pillar, but public skepticism about nuclear technologies—rooted in historical incidents like Chernobyl and Fukushima—remains a barrier. The EIIR advocates for "public trust and transparency," recommending that governments and organizations adopt participatory governance models that involve local communities in decision-making processes.

The role of civil society and media in shaping fusion narratives cannot be overlooked. The EIIR’s symposium on the Russian-Ukrainian war, while not directly related to fusion, highlights the broader geopolitical risks of energy dependencies. In the context of fusion, similar tensions could arise if critical components, such as rare earth metals for superconducting magnets, become concentrated in politically unstable regions. The EIIR calls for diversifying supply chains and fostering international agreements to prevent energy technologies from becoming geopolitical flashpoints.

### Conclusion

The geopolitics of nuclear fusion is a complex interplay of technical, ethical, and strategic factors. The EIIR’s research underscores that fusion’s success hinges not only on scientific breakthroughs but also on robust governance frameworks, equitable resource distribution, and public engagement. As projects like ITER and private ventures race toward commercialization, the international community must navigate the delicate balance between innovation and responsibility. By addressing challenges such as tritium management, non-proliferation, and global equity, fusion energy can fulfill its promise as a transformative force for a sustainable and geopolitically stable future.
