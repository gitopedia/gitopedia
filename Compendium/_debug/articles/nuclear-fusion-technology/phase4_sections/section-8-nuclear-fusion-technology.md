## Geopolitics of Nuclear Fusion

Nuclear fusion, the process of combining atomic nuclei to release vast amounts of energy, has emerged as a focal point in global energy and security strategies. Unlike fission-based nuclear energy, which relies on splitting atoms and carries risks of meltdowns and proliferation, fusion is often touted as a safer, cleaner, and nearly inexhaustible energy source. Its potential to reshape energy markets, reduce reliance on fossil fuels, and address climate change has sparked intense geopolitical competition and collaboration among nations. This section examines the evolving geopolitical dynamics of nuclear fusion, focusing on international collaborations, resource politics, institutional contributions, and future implications.

### International Collaborations and Competitions

Nuclear fusion has become a cornerstone of global energy diplomacy, with major projects serving as both unifying and contentious platforms. The **International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor (ITER)**, launched in 1985 and operational since 2025 in southern France, is a prime example of multilateral cooperation. Funded by 35 nations including the United States, China, India, Russia, and members of the European Union, ITER aims to demonstrate the feasibility of sustained fusion reactions. However, the project has faced delays and cost overruns (initially estimated at €5 billion, now exceeding €25 billion), reflecting the complexities of coordinating diverse national interests. While ITER fosters scientific collaboration, it also highlights geopolitical tensions, such as Russia’s 2022 suspension of contributions to the project due to Western sanctions over the Ukraine war, underscoring how fusion diplomacy is intertwined with broader international relations.

In contrast to multilateral efforts, nations are also pursuing unilateral advancements. The **United States** has prioritized fusion through the Department of Energy’s $500 million investment in private-sector initiatives like General Atomics and Helion Energy, aiming for commercial deployment by the 2030s. **China** has made significant strides with its Experimental Advanced Superconducting Tokamak (EAST) reactor, achieving record-breaking plasma temperatures and durations, positioning itself as a global leader in fusion research. The **European Union**, through Horizon Europe, has allocated €10 billion to fusion projects, emphasizing energy independence amid the fallout from the Russian gas crisis. Meanwhile, **private firms** such as UK-based Tokamak Energy and Canada’s CTFusion are accelerating innovation, raising questions about how publicly and privately developed fusion technologies might intersect in the future.

### Resource Geopolitics and Strategic Competition

The geopolitics of fusion extends beyond reactors to the critical resources required for fuel, infrastructure, and technology. **Lithium**, essential for producing tritium (a fusion fuel), is a key focal point. Australia, Chile, and Argentina control over 70% of global lithium reserves, creating a potential bottleneck for fusion development. Similarly, **helium-3**, a rare isotope ideal for advanced fusion reactions, is predominantly found in lunar regolith, prompting speculative discussions about space mining and its geopolitical ramifications. While current fusion projects rely on deuterium (extracted from seawater) and lithium-based tritium breeding, the long-term viability of these fuels hinges on securing stable supply chains, which could exacerbate resource competition.

**Rare earth elements** (REEs), crucial for superconducting magnets in tokamaks and stellarators, further complicate the landscape. China dominates 90% of global rare earth processing, giving it significant leverage over fusion technology development. The **European Institute for International Law and International Relations (EIIR)** has highlighted how resource dependencies could replicate existing fossil fuel dynamics, with nations forming strategic alliances or rivalries over access to critical materials. For instance, the EU’s Green Deal emphasizes decoupling from Chinese rare earth processing, a move that could indirectly influence fusion energy strategies.

### Institutional Contributions and Policy Frameworks

The **EIIR** plays a pivotal role in analyzing fusion’s geopolitical implications, integrating legal, strategic, and policy perspectives. Its **Solidarity4All project (2024–2026)**, funded by the European Commission, explores how fusion energy could strengthen EU solidarity through shared infrastructure and knowledge transfer, particularly in addressing energy poverty in marginalized regions. The institute’s research also examines fusion’s role in **EU enlargement strategies**, assessing how energy cooperation might facilitate Ukraine’s integration into European energy networks, thereby countering Russian influence.

The EIIR has also scrutinized **China’s growing influence in Africa**, questioning whether Beijing’s investments in infrastructure and resources—such as lithium extraction in the Democratic Republic of Congo—constitute a new form of colonialism or a model of mutual benefit. This analysis is critical for understanding how fusion’s resource demands could reshape global power dynamics. Additionally, the institute’s 2022 symposium on the **end of the Russian-Ukrainian war** underscored fusion’s potential as a neutralizing force in energy geopolitics, proposing that post-war energy collaborations could foster regional stability.

### Future Implications and Geopolitical Scenarios

As fusion technology advances, its geopolitical impact will depend on how nations manage its deployment. A **collaborative scenario** could see fusion as a tool for global energy equity, with international bodies like the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) regulating technology sharing to prevent monopolization. Conversely, a **competitive scenario** might lead to a "fusion arms race," where nations hoard resources, restrict intellectual property, and weaponize energy security. The **European Green Deal’s emphasis on fusion** as part of a decarbonized energy mix could serve as a model for multilateral governance, though success hinges on overcoming technical and political hurdles.

The EIIR’s research also warns of **regional disparities** in fusion adoption. Wealthier nations may achieve commercial fusion first, exacerbating energy inequalities, while developing countries could face exclusion from the technology. This dynamic mirrors historical patterns in renewable energy access, such as the uneven distribution of solar panel manufacturing. Furthermore, fusion’s potential to reduce fossil fuel dependence might destabilize oil-rich economies, prompting geopolitical recalibrations in regions like the Middle East and Africa.

### Conclusion

The geopolitics of nuclear fusion is a multifaceted arena where scientific ambition intersects with resource scarcity, institutional diplomacy, and strategic competition. While international projects like ITER and the EU’s Green Deal offer pathways to cooperation, the race for resources and technological leadership risks replicating the tensions of fossil fuel geopolitics. Institutions such as the EIIR are critical in navigating these challenges, advocating for frameworks that balance innovation with equity. As fusion moves closer to commercialization, its geopolitical trajectory will be shaped by whether nations prioritize shared prosperity or national advantage—a choice with profound implications for global energy security and climate stability.
