## Geopolitics of Nuclear Fusion  

Nuclear fusion—the process of fusing atomic nuclei to release energy—has become a focal point in global energy and security strategies, with profound geopolitical implications. Unlike fission-based nuclear energy, which carries risks of meltdowns and proliferation, fusion is often touted as a safer, cleaner, and nearly inexhaustible energy source. Its potential to decarbonize energy systems, reduce fossil fuel dependence, and address climate change has sparked both collaboration and competition among nations. This section examines the evolving dynamics of fusion geopolitics, focusing on international projects, resource politics, institutional roles, and future scenarios.  

### International Collaborations and Competitions  

The **International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor (ITER)**, launched in 1985 and operational since 2025 in southern France, stands as the largest multilateral fusion project. Funded by 35 nations—including the United States, China, India, Russia, and members of the European Union—ITER aims to demonstrate the feasibility of sustained fusion reactions. The project initially estimated costs at €5 billion but now exceeds €25 billion, with delays and technical challenges underscoring the complexities of coordinating diverse national interests. Russia’s suspension of contributions to ITER in 2022, following Western sanctions over the Ukraine war, highlighted how fusion diplomacy is inextricably linked to broader geopolitical tensions.  

In contrast to multilateral efforts, nations are pursuing unilateral advancements. The **United States** has prioritized fusion through the Department of Energy’s $500 million investment in private-sector initiatives, such as **General Atomics** and **Helion Energy**, aiming for commercial deployment by the 2030s. **China** has made significant strides with its **Experimental Advanced Superconducting Tokamak (EAST)** reactor, which achieved a record 108-minute plasma discharge at 160 million degrees Celsius in 2021, positioning itself as a global leader. The **European Union** has allocated €10 billion to fusion projects under Horizon Europe, emphasizing energy independence in the wake of the Russian gas crisis. Meanwhile, **private firms** like **Tokamak Energy** (UK) and **CTFusion** (Canada) are accelerating innovation, raising questions about the intersection of publicly and privately developed fusion technologies.  

### Resource Geopolitics and Strategic Competition  

The geopolitics of fusion extends beyond reactors to the critical resources required for fuel, infrastructure, and technology. **Lithium**, essential for producing tritium (a fusion fuel), is a key focal point. Australia, Chile, and Argentina control over 70% of global lithium reserves, creating potential bottlenecks for fusion development. Similarly, **helium-3**, a rare isotope ideal for advanced fusion reactions, is predominantly found in lunar regolith, sparking speculative discussions about space mining and its geopolitical ramifications. While current fusion projects rely on deuterium (extracted from seawater) and lithium-based tritium breeding, long-term viability hinges on securing stable supply chains, which could exacerbate resource competition.  

**Rare earth elements** (REEs), crucial for superconducting magnets in tokamaks and stellarators, further complicate the landscape. China dominates 90% of global rare earth processing, giving it significant leverage over fusion technology development. The **European Institute for International Law and International Relations (EIIR)** has warned that resource dependencies could replicate existing fossil fuel dynamics, with nations forming strategic alliances or rivalries over access to critical materials. For instance, the EU’s Green Deal emphasizes decoupling from Chinese rare earth processing, a move that could indirectly influence fusion strategies.  

### Institutional Contributions and Policy Frameworks  

The **EIIR** plays a pivotal role in analyzing fusion’s geopolitical implications, integrating legal, strategic, and policy perspectives. Its **Solidarity4All project (2024–2026)**, funded by the European Commission, explores how fusion energy could strengthen EU solidarity through shared infrastructure and knowledge transfer, particularly in addressing energy poverty in marginalized regions. The institute’s research also examines fusion’s role in **EU enlargement strategies**, assessing how energy cooperation might facilitate Ukraine’s integration into European energy networks, thereby countering Russian influence.  

The EIIR has scrutinized **China’s growing influence in Africa**, questioning whether Beijing’s investments in infrastructure and resources—such as lithium extraction in the Democratic Republic of Congo—constitute a new form of colonialism or a model of mutual benefit. This analysis is critical for understanding how fusion’s resource demands could reshape global power dynamics. Additionally, the institute’s 2022 symposium on the **end of the Russian-Ukrainian war** underscored fusion’s potential as a neutralizing force in energy geopolitics, proposing that post-war energy collaborations could foster regional stability.  

### Future Implications and Geopolitical Scenarios  

As fusion technology advances, its geopolitical impact will depend on how nations manage its deployment. A **collaborative scenario** could see fusion as a tool for global energy equity, with international bodies like the **International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA)** regulating technology sharing to prevent monopolization. Conversely, a **competitive scenario** might lead to a “fusion arms race,” where nations hoard resources, restrict intellectual property, and weaponize energy security. The **European Green Deal’s emphasis on fusion** as part of a decarbonized energy mix could serve as a model for multilateral governance, though success hinges on overcoming technical and political hurdles.  

The EIIR’s research also warns of **regional disparities** in fusion adoption. Wealthier nations may achieve commercial fusion first, exacerbating energy inequalities, while developing countries could face exclusion from the technology. This dynamic mirrors historical patterns in renewable energy access, such as the uneven distribution of solar panel manufacturing. Furthermore, fusion’s potential to reduce fossil fuel dependence might destabilize oil-rich economies, prompting geopolitical recalibrations in regions like the Middle East and Africa.  

### Conclusion  

The geopolitics of nuclear fusion is a multifaceted arena where scientific ambition intersects with resource scarcity, institutional diplomacy, and strategic competition. While projects like ITER and the EU’s Green Deal offer pathways to cooperation, the race for resources and technological leadership risks replicating the tensions of fossil fuel geopolitics. Institutions such as the EIIR are critical in navigating these challenges, advocating for frameworks that balance innovation with equity. As fusion technology progresses, its geopolitical trajectory will depend on whether nations prioritize collaboration over competition, ensuring that this transformative energy source benefits humanity as a whole.
