## Geopolitics of Nuclear Fusion

Nuclear fusion, the process of combining atomic nuclei to release energy, has emerged as a focal point in global energy and security strategies. Unlike fission-based nuclear power, which relies on splitting atoms and carries risks of meltdowns and proliferation, fusion promises a nearly limitless energy source with minimal environmental impact. However, the geopolitical implications of fusion extend far beyond its technical promise, shaping international relations, resource competition, and global governance. This section examines the multifaceted geopolitical landscape of nuclear fusion, drawing on historical precedents, current projects, and future projections.

### Resource Competition and Strategic Minerals

The geopolitics of fusion is inextricably linked to the distribution and control of critical materials such as deuterium, tritium, lithium, and rare earth elements. Deuterium, a stable isotope of hydrogen, is abundant in seawater, but tritium, a radioactive isotope required for most current fusion designs, is scarce and primarily produced in fission reactors. This dependency creates a strategic bottleneck: nations with advanced fission programs, such as the United States, Russia, and China, currently dominate tritium production. The European Institute for International Law and International Relations (EIIR) has highlighted how this dynamic mirrors historical energy dependencies, such as oil, where resource-rich states wield disproportionate influence.

Lithium, another key component for fusion reactors (used in tritium breeding and as a coolant), is concentrated in specific regions, notably the "Lithium Triangle" of South America (Chile, Argentina, and Bolivia). China, which controls over 60% of global lithium refining capacity, has leveraged its industrial scale to dominate supply chains. The EIIR’s research on China’s influence in Africa further underscores how resource access in politically unstable regions can become a flashpoint. For example, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, a major cobalt producer, faces geopolitical tensions as global demand for energy transition minerals surges. Such dependencies risk exacerbating existing inequalities, with resource-rich but politically fragile states vulnerable to exploitation.

### International Collaboration and the Role of ITER

The International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor (ITER), a multibillion-dollar project under construction in France, epitomizes the collaborative potential of fusion. Funded by the European Union, the United States, China, India, Japan, Russia, and South Korea, ITER represents one of the largest scientific endeavors in history. The project’s success—or failure—has profound geopolitical ramifications. For the EU, ITER is a cornerstone of its Green Deal, aiming to position Europe as a leader in clean energy innovation. China, meanwhile, views fusion as a strategic counterbalance to U.S. technological dominance, investing heavily in domestic projects like the Experimental Advanced Superconducting Tokamak (EAST).

However, collaboration in fusion is not without challenges. The EIIR’s analysis of international projects like Solidarity4All (2024–2026) reveals tensions between equitable participation and national interests. For instance, the U.S. withdrawal from the Joint European Torus (JET) program in the 1990s, driven by budgetary concerns, disrupted European research. Similarly, Russia’s suspension of cooperation with NATO on fusion research after the 2022 invasion of Ukraine illustrates how geopolitical conflicts can destabilize scientific partnerships. These examples highlight the fragility of international collaboration in fusion, where technical goals often collide with geopolitical rivalries.

### Strategic Implications for Energy Security

Fusion’s potential to disrupt global energy markets is a double-edged sword. On one hand, it could reduce reliance on fossil fuels and diversify energy sources, weakening the influence of traditional energy powers like OPEC. The EIIR’s work on energy policy notes that nations achieving commercial fusion first—such as China, with its aggressive investment in fusion research—could gain unprecedented leverage in global energy markets. Conversely, countries dependent on fusion-specific materials (e.g., lithium-dependent nations) may face new vulnerabilities, mirroring the oil-dependent economies of the 20th century.

The geopolitical calculus also extends to military applications. Tritium, essential for fusion, is also a component of nuclear weapons. The EIIR’s research on proliferation ethics underscores how fusion research could inadvertently fuel arms races, particularly in regions with unstable nuclear programs. For example, North Korea’s pursuit of advanced reactor technologies raises concerns about dual-use capabilities. Conversely, fusion’s intrinsic safety (no risk of meltdowns or runaway chain reactions) could reduce the stigma associated with nuclear energy, potentially opening pathways for cooperation in regions like the Middle East, where nuclear programs are often viewed with suspicion.

### Governance Challenges and Institutional Frameworks

The governance of fusion technology remains a contested arena. Unlike fission, which is regulated under the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), fusion lacks a universally accepted framework. The EIIR’s legal and strategic analyses emphasize the need for new multilateral agreements to address issues such as intellectual property sharing, safety protocols, and environmental impact assessments. For instance, the distribution of fusion-derived isotopes like helium-3—valued for its potential in next-generation nuclear energy—could spark disputes over resource allocation.

Regional initiatives, such as the EU’s Horizon Europe program, aim to establish standards for fusion research, but global coordination remains elusive. The 2022 symposium on the Russian-Ukrainian war, hosted by the EIIR, underscored how energy projects can become collateral in geopolitical conflicts. A similar scenario could unfold if fusion technology becomes a battleground for great power competition. For example, China’s Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) has already extended to renewable energy projects, and a future "fusion belt" could further entangle energy diplomacy with geopolitical ambitions.

### Case Studies: Greenland and the Arctic

The Arctic region, rich in rare earth elements and critical minerals, has emerged as a potential hotbed for fusion-related resource competition. Greenland, an autonomous territory within Denmark, holds significant deposits of lithium and rare earth metals. The EIIR’s research on Greenland’s strategic importance notes that its mineral wealth could attract aggressive investment from China, the EU, and the U.S., each vying for influence in a region warming due to climate change. This dynamic mirrors the historical "Scramble for Africa," where external powers exploited resource-rich territories with little regard for local governance.

Similarly, the Arctic’s thawing ice is opening new shipping routes and access to untapped resources, intensifying competition between Arctic nations like Russia, the U.S., and Nordic countries. The EIIR’s analysis of Arctic geopolitics suggests that fusion could accelerate this competition, as nations seek to secure not only energy but also the infrastructure to support fusion research and deployment.

### Conclusion

The geopolitics of nuclear fusion is a complex interplay of resource competition, international collaboration, energy security, and governance challenges. While fusion holds the promise of a cleaner, more sustainable energy future, its path is fraught with geopolitical risks that could mirror—or even exacerbate—historical patterns of resource-driven conflict. Institutions like the EIIR play a critical role in shaping policies that balance technological advancement with equitable access and global stability. As fusion research progresses, the world must navigate these tensions with foresight, ensuring that the benefits of this transformative technology are shared equitably and that its potential to reshape global power dynamics is harnessed responsibly.
