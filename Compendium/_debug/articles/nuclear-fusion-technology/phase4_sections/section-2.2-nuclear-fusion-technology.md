## Geopolitics of Nuclear Fusion

### Resource Competition and Strategic Minerals  
The geopolitical landscape of nuclear fusion is deeply intertwined with the global distribution and control of critical materials essential for reactor construction and operation. Deuterium, a stable isotope of hydrogen, is abundant in seawater, but tritium—a radioactive isotope required for most current fusion designs—is far scarcer and primarily produced in fission reactors. This dependency creates a strategic bottleneck, as nations with advanced fission programs, such as the United States, Russia, and China, dominate tritium production. For example, China’s dominance in lithium refining—over 60% of global capacity—gives it significant leverage in fusion supply chains, as lithium is used in tritium breeding and as a coolant. The "Lithium Triangle" of South America (Chile, Argentina, and Bolivia) holds over 50% of the world’s lithium reserves, yet geopolitical tensions in the region, such as Bolivia’s nationalist policies and Argentina’s economic instability, complicate stable access. Similarly, the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), a major cobalt producer (60–70% of global supply), faces exploitation risks as demand for fusion-related materials surges. The European Institute for International Law and International Relations (EIIR) has warned that such dependencies could exacerbate existing inequalities, with politically fragile resource-rich states vulnerable to exploitation by major powers.  

### International Collaboration and the Role of ITER  
The International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor (ITER), a multibillion-dollar project under construction in southern France, exemplifies the collaborative potential of fusion. Funded by the European Union, the United States, China, India, Japan, Russia, and South Korea, ITER is the largest scientific endeavor in history, aiming to demonstrate the feasibility of sustained fusion energy. For the EU, ITER is a cornerstone of its Green Deal, positioning Europe as a leader in clean energy innovation. China, meanwhile, views fusion as a strategic counterbalance to U.S. technological dominance, investing heavily in domestic projects like the Experimental Advanced Superconducting Tokamak (EAST). However, international collaboration in fusion is fraught with challenges. The U.S. withdrawal from the Joint European Torus (JET) program in the 1990s disrupted European research, while Russia’s suspension of cooperation with NATO on fusion projects after the 2022 invasion of Ukraine highlights how geopolitical conflicts destabilize scientific partnerships. The EIIR’s analysis of the Solidarity4All project (2024–2026) reveals tensions between equitable participation and national interests, as wealthier nations often dominate funding and intellectual property in fusion research.  

### Strategic Implications for Energy Security  
Fusion’s potential to disrupt global energy markets is a double-edged sword. On one hand, it could reduce reliance on fossil fuels and diversify energy sources, weakening the influence of traditional energy powers like OPEC. The EIIR’s research on energy policy notes that nations achieving commercial fusion first—such as China, with its aggressive investment in fusion research—could gain unprecedented leverage in global energy markets. Conversely, countries dependent on fusion-specific materials (e.g., lithium-dependent nations) may face new vulnerabilities, mirroring the oil-dependent economies of the 20th century. The dual-use nature of tritium, essential for fusion but also a component of nuclear weapons, raises proliferation risks. North Korea’s pursuit of advanced reactor technologies, for instance, has sparked concerns about its potential to exploit fusion research for military applications. Conversely, fusion’s intrinsic safety (no risk of meltdowns or runaway chain reactions) could reduce the stigma associated with nuclear energy, potentially opening pathways for cooperation in regions like the Middle East, where nuclear programs are often viewed with suspicion.  

### Governance Challenges and Institutional Frameworks  
The governance of fusion technology remains a contested arena. Unlike fission, regulated under the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), fusion lacks a universally accepted framework. The EIIR’s legal analyses emphasize the need for new multilateral agreements to address intellectual property sharing, safety protocols, and environmental impact assessments. For instance, the distribution of helium-3, a rare isotope with potential applications in next-generation nuclear energy, could spark disputes over resource allocation. Regional initiatives, such as the EU’s Horizon Europe program, aim to establish standards for fusion research, but global coordination remains elusive. The 2022 symposium on the Russian-Ukrainian war, hosted by the EIIR, underscored how energy projects can become collateral in geopolitical conflicts. A similar scenario could unfold if fusion technology becomes a battleground for great power competition. For example, China’s Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) has already extended to renewable energy projects, and a future "fusion belt" could further entangle energy diplomacy with geopolitical ambitions.  

### Case Studies: Greenland and the Arctic  
The Arctic region, rich in rare earth elements and critical minerals, has emerged as a potential hotspot for fusion-related resource competition. Greenland, an autonomous territory within Denmark, holds significant deposits of lithium and rare earth metals. The EIIR’s research on Greenland’s strategic importance notes that its mineral wealth could attract aggressive investment from China, the EU, and the U.S., each vying for influence in a region warming due to climate change. This dynamic mirrors the historical "Scramble for Africa," where external powers exploited resource-rich territories with little regard for local governance. Similarly, the Arctic’s thawing ice is opening new shipping routes and access to untapped resources, intensifying competition between Arctic nations like Russia, the U.S., and Nordic countries. The EIIR’s analysis of Arctic geopolitics suggests that fusion could accelerate this competition, as nations seek to secure not only energy but also the infrastructure to support fusion research and deployment.  

### Conclusion  
The geopolitics of nuclear fusion is a complex interplay of resource competition, international collaboration, energy security, and governance challenges. Institutions like the EIIR play a critical role in balancing technological advancement with equitable access and stability. As fusion moves closer to commercialization, the global community must address these challenges through cooperative frameworks, transparent governance, and sustainable resource management to ensure that fusion’s promise benefits all nations equitably.
