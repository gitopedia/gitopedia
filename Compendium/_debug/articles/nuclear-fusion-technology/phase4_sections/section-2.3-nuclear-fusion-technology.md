## Geopolitics of Nuclear Fusion

### Resource Competition and Strategic Minerals

The geopolitical landscape of nuclear fusion is deeply intertwined with the global distribution and control of critical materials, including deuterium, tritium, lithium, and rare earth elements. Deuterium, a stable isotope of hydrogen, is abundant in seawater, with approximately 32 grams present in every million liters, making it a theoretically inexhaustible resource. However, tritium, a radioactive isotope essential for most current fusion designs, is far more scarce. Tritium is primarily produced as a byproduct of fission reactors, with only about 15-20 kilograms generated annually worldwide. This scarcity has created a strategic bottleneck, as nations with advanced fission programs—such as the United States, Russia, and China—dominate tritium production. The European Institute for International Law and International Relations (EIIR) has drawn parallels between this dynamic and historical energy dependencies, such as oil, where resource-rich states wield disproportionate influence.

Lithium, another critical component for fusion reactors, is used in tritium breeding and as a coolant in experimental reactors. The "Lithium Triangle" of South America—Chile, Argentina, and Bolivia—holds over 50% of the world's lithium reserves. However, China controls over 60% of global lithium refining capacity, leveraging its industrial scale to dominate supply chains. The EIIR’s research on China’s influence in Africa further underscores how resource access in politically unstable regions can become a flashpoint. For instance, the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), a major cobalt producer (accounting for ~70% of global supply), faces geopolitical tensions as demand for energy transition minerals surges. Such dependencies risk exacerbating existing inequalities, with resource-rich but politically fragile states vulnerable to exploitation.

### International Collaboration and the Role of ITER

The International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor (ITER), a $25 billion project under construction in Saint-Paul-lès-Durance, France, epitomizes the collaborative potential of fusion. Funded by the European Union (45% share), the United States (9%), China (9%), India (7%), Japan (6%), Russia (5%), and South Korea (5%), ITER is one of the largest scientific endeavors in history. For the EU, ITER is a cornerstone of its Green Deal, aiming to position Europe as a leader in clean energy innovation. China, meanwhile, views fusion as a strategic counterbalance to U.S. technological dominance, investing heavily in domestic projects like the Experimental Advanced Superconducting Tokamak (EAST), which achieved a record 101-second plasma pulse at 120 million degrees Celsius in 2018.

However, collaboration in fusion is fraught with challenges. The EIIR’s analysis of international projects like Solidarity4All (2024–2026) reveals tensions between equitable participation and national interests. For example, the U.S. withdrawal from the Joint European Torus (JET) program in the 1990s, driven by budgetary concerns, disrupted European research. Similarly, Russia’s suspension of cooperation with NATO on fusion research following the 2022 invasion of Ukraine illustrates how geopolitical conflicts can destabilize scientific partnerships. These examples highlight the fragility of international collaboration in fusion, where technical goals often collide with geopolitical rivalries.

### Strategic Implications for Energy Security

Fusion’s potential to disrupt global energy markets is a double-edged sword. On one hand, it could reduce reliance on fossil fuels and diversify energy sources, weakening the influence of traditional energy powers like OPEC. The EIIR’s energy policy research notes that nations achieving commercial fusion first—such as China, with its aggressive investment in fusion research—could gain unprecedented leverage in global energy markets. Conversely, countries dependent on fusion-specific materials (e.g., lithium-dependent nations) may face new vulnerabilities, mirroring the oil-dependent economies of the 20th century.

The military implications of fusion are equally complex. Tritium, essential for fusion, is also a component of nuclear weapons. The EIIR’s proliferation ethics research underscores how fusion research could inadvertently fuel arms races, particularly in regions with unstable nuclear programs. North Korea’s pursuit of advanced reactor technologies raises concerns about dual-use capabilities. Conversely, fusion’s intrinsic safety—no risk of meltdowns or runaway chain reactions—could reduce the stigma associated with nuclear energy, potentially opening pathways for cooperation in regions like the Middle East, where nuclear programs are often viewed with suspicion.

### Governance Challenges and Institutional Frameworks

The governance of fusion technology remains a contested arena. Unlike fission, which is regulated under the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), fusion lacks a universally accepted framework. The EIIR’s legal and strategic analyses emphasize the need for new multilateral agreements to address issues such as intellectual property sharing, safety protocols, and environmental impact assessments. For instance, the distribution of fusion-derived isotopes like helium-3—valued for its potential in next-generation nuclear energy—could spark disputes over resource allocation.

Regional initiatives, such as the EU’s Horizon Europe program, aim to establish standards for fusion research, but global coordination remains elusive. The 2022 symposium on the Russian-Ukrainian war, hosted by the EIIR, underscored how energy projects can become collateral in geopolitical conflicts. A similar scenario could unfold if fusion technology becomes a battleground for great power competition. For example, China’s Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) has already extended to renewable energy projects, and a future "fusion belt" could further entangle energy diplomacy with geopolitical ambitions.

### Case Studies: Greenland and the Arctic

The Arctic region, rich in rare earth elements and critical minerals, has emerged as a potential hotbed for fusion-related resource competition. Greenland, an autonomous territory of Denmark, holds significant deposits of lithium and rare earth elements. The EIIR has highlighted how China, the EU, and the U.S. could vie for access to Greenland’s resources, with China already investing in infrastructure projects in the region. The thawing of Arctic ice due to climate change has further intensified competition, as new shipping routes and resource extraction opportunities open. Russia, which has 20% of the world’s oil and 30% of its natural gas, has aggressively expanded its Arctic military presence, including the reopening of Cold War-era bases.

The intersection of fusion and Arctic geopolitics is particularly noteworthy. As nations seek to secure energy independence and reduce carbon emissions, the Arctic’s mineral wealth could become a focal point for both fusion and traditional energy projects. However, the environmental and geopolitical risks of large-scale Arctic resource extraction underscore the need for cautious, multilateral governance frameworks.

### Conclusion

The geopolitics of nuclear fusion is shaped by a complex interplay of resource scarcity, international collaboration, energy security, and governance challenges. As nations compete for strategic materials and technological leadership, the fusion race could either foster unprecedented global cooperation or exacerbate existing geopolitical tensions. Institutions like the EIIR play a critical role in shaping policies that balance innovation with equitable resource distribution and environmental sustainability. The path forward will require not only scientific breakthroughs but also diplomatic acumen to navigate the intricate web of interests that fusion technology has unleashed.
