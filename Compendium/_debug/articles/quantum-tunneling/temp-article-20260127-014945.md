# Quantum Tunneling

## Overview
Quantum tunneling is a phenomenon that occurs in quantum mechanics, where particles pass through potential barriers that they classically should not be able to overcome due to insufficient energy. This effect challenges classical intuition and has significant applications in various fields of physics and technology.

## Transmission Coefficient
The transmission coefficient \( T \) describes the probability of a particle tunneling through a potential barrier. For an electron encountering a one-dimensional barrier, it is given by:

\[
T = \left(1 + \frac{V_0^2}{4E(V_0 + E)} \sin^2\left(\frac{L}{\hbar} \sqrt{2m(V_0 + E)}\right)\right)^{-1}
\]

where:
- \( V_0 = 3 \, \text{eV} \) is the barrier height,
- \( E = 1 \, \text{eV} \) is the electron energy,
- \( L = 1 \, \text{nm} \) is the barrier width.

This formula quantifies how likely a particle is to pass through the barrier based on its parameters.

## Gamow Model of Radioactive Decay
The transmission probability for alpha decay, as described by the Gamow model, is:

\[
T = e^{-2\gamma}
\]

where \( \gamma \) is defined as:

\[
\gamma = \frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar} \left(\frac{\pi}{2} r_1 - 2\sqrt{r_0 r_1}\right)
\]

Here, \( r_0 \) and \( r_1 \) are the radii where the energy \( E \) intersects the potential \( V(r) \). This model provides a quantum mechanical explanation for radioactive decay.

## Applications of Quantum Tunneling
Quantum tunneling has numerous practical applications:

- **Scanning Tunneling Microscopy (STM):** Maps surface topography at atomic scales by measuring current flow between a metal needle and a sample.
- **Tunnel Diodes:** Allow unidirectional current flow, useful in high-speed electronic devices.
- **Josephson Junctions:** Enable superconducting currents through thin insulating barriers via Cooper pair tunneling.

## Impact of Potential Shift on Alpha Decay Probability
A shift in the equation for \( \gamma \) by \( \ln 2 / 2 \) alters the transmission probability:

\[
T \propto e^{-2(\gamma - \frac{\ln 2}{2})} = e^{-2\gamma + \ln 2}
\]

This results in a factor change of \( e^{\ln 2} = 2 \), effectively halving the alpha decay probability.
