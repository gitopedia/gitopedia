# Quantum Entanglement

## Overview
Quantum entanglement is a fundamental phenomenon in quantum mechanics where particles become interconnected such that the state of one particle instantaneously influences the state of another, regardless of distance. This property has been pivotal in advancing precision measurements and has been experimentally confirmed through various studies.

## History
The concept of quantum entanglement was first discussed in the Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen (EPR) paradox, which questioned the completeness of quantum mechanics. However, experimental confirmation of the EPR paradox, particularly through the work of Alain Aspect in the 1980s, demonstrated that measurements on entangled objects remain strongly linked despite spatial separation. This breakthrough was recognized with the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics.

## Key Concepts
Quantum metrology is an emerging field that leverages quantum effects, particularly entanglement, to enhance measurement precision. By using entangled systems, researchers can achieve higher accuracy in measuring physical quantities such as electromagnetic fields and gravitational acceleration. For instance, spatially separated entangled atoms have been shown to measure multiple physical parameters with improved precision.

## Applications
### Quantum Metrology
Quantum metrology applies quantum principles to improve the accuracy of measurements. Optical lattice clocks, for example, use entangled atomic spins to achieve highly precise timekeeping. These systems are less susceptible to errors caused by atom distribution within lattices due to entanglement. Additionally, atom interferometers benefiting from entanglement have been used to measure Earth's gravitational acceleration with greater precision.

### Practical Uses
The techniques developed in quantum metrology have practical applications in fields such as geophysics and timekeeping. For instance, researchers at the University of Basel and Laboratoire Kastler Brossel demonstrated that spatially separated entangled systems could determine field distributions with higher accuracy than traditional methods. These advancements also hold promise for enhancing optical lattice clocks and gravimeters.
