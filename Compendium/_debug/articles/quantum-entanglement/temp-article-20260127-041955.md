# Quantum Entanglement

## Overview
Quantum entanglement is a phenomenon in quantum mechanics where particles become correlated in such a way that the state of one particle instantaneously influences the state of another, regardless of the distance separating them. This challenges classical intuitions about locality and has profound implications for our understanding of physics.

## History
The concept of entanglement was first introduced by Albert Einstein, Boris Podolsky, and Nathan Rosen in their 1935 paper, which highlighted what Einstein described as "spooky action at a distance." This term reflected Einstein's discomfort with the nonlocal nature of quantum mechanics. Decades later, John Stewart Bell advanced the understanding of entanglement through his 1964 theorem, demonstrating that quantum mechanics predicts stronger correlations than any local theory could explain.

## Key Concepts
Entanglement involves particles losing their independence and exhibiting highly correlated measurement outcomes. When the spin or other properties of one entangled particle are measured, the state of the entangled partner is instantly determined, regardless of the distance between them. This nonlocal correlation defies classical explanations based on local hidden variables.

## Local Hidden Variables
Einstein proposed local hidden variables as an alternative explanation for entanglement, suggesting that particles have preexisting values (hidden variables) that determine their measurement outcomes without requiring nonlocal influence. However, Bell's theorem demonstrated that such theories could not fully account for the statistical correlations predicted by quantum mechanics.

## Bell’s Theorem
Bell's theorem mathematically proved that local hidden variable theories could not explain all possible measurement outcomes in quantum systems. Specifically, Bell showed that if locality held, the probability of obtaining opposite results when measuring particles along different axes would be capped at 67% (as per Bell's inequality). Quantum mechanics, however, predicts higher correlations, such as 75% same results when the measurement axes are set at 120 degrees apart.

## Experimental Validation
Since the 1970s, experiments have consistently supported quantum mechanics predictions over local hidden variable theories. These experiments closed various loopholes, confirming that nonlocality is an inherent aspect of our universe. The experimental confirmation of Bell's theorem has been a landmark in quantum physics.

## Impact of Bell’s Theorem
Bell’s theorem revolutionized the understanding of quantum mechanics, challenging deeply held intuitions about locality and causality. It paved the way for advancements in quantum technologies, including quantum computing and secure communication protocols. Furthermore, it opened new avenues for exploring nonlocality and its implications for our classical understanding of the physical world.
