# Quantum Entanglement

## Overview
Quantum entanglement is a fundamental phenomenon in quantum mechanics where two or more particles become linked in such a way that the state of one particle instantly influences the state of another, regardless of the distance separating them. This connection defies classical intuitions about locality and realism, highlighting the unique nature of quantum reality.

## History
The concept of entanglement was first introduced by physicist Albert Einstein, who, along with Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen, formulated the EPR paradox in 1935. They argued that quantum mechanics might be incomplete due to the non-local correlations observed in entangled particles, which they referred to as "spooky action at a distance." This critique was addressed by physicist John Bell, who developed Bell's theorem in 1964. Bell's theorem provided a mathematical framework to test whether local hidden variables could explain quantum phenomena. Experiments, notably those conducted by Alain Aspect in the 1980s, demonstrated that the correlations violated Bell's inequalities, confirming the non-local nature of entanglement and supporting the completeness of quantum mechanics.

## Key Concepts
Entanglement arises when two or more particles are generated or interact such that their quantum states become correlated. Before measurement, each particle exists in a superposition of possible states. When one particle's state is measured, the other's state collapses to a corresponding definite value instantaneously, regardless of distance. This phenomenon was famously described by Einstein as "spooky action at a distance."

## Applications in Quantum Computing
Entanglement is a cornerstone of quantum computing, enabling qubits (quantum bits) to perform computations that are intractable for classical computers. Unlike classical bits, which can be either 0 or 1, qubits leverage superposition to exist in both states simultaneously. Entangled qubits exponentially increase computational power by allowing multiple calculations to occur in parallel. This interconnectedness is harnessed through quantum gates like the Controlled-NOT gate, enabling tasks such as factoring large numbers and simulating molecular interactions.

## Challenges
A major obstacle to practical quantum computing is decoherence, where entangled states are disrupted by environmental interference. Decoherence occurs due to interactions with external factors like electromagnetic fields or temperature fluctuations, causing the collapse of superposition and entanglement. To mitigate this, quantum systems require highly controlled environments, such as near-absolute-zero temperatures or ultra-high vacuums, presenting significant engineering challenges for developing stable quantum hardware.

By understanding and overcoming these challenges, entanglement promises to revolutionize computing and technology, unlocking unprecedented computational capabilities.
