# Quantum Entanglement

## Overview
Quantum entanglement is a fundamental phenomenon in quantum mechanics where pairs or groups of particles become interconnected such that the state of each particle cannot be described independently of the others, even when separated by large distances. This connection persists regardless of distance, challenging classical notions of locality and influencing various fields, including quantum computing and cryptography.

## History
The concept was first introduced in 1935 by Albert Einstein, Boris Podolsky, and Nathan Rosen through the Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen (EPR) paradox. The EPR paradox questioned the completeness of quantum mechanics, suggesting the existence of "hidden variables" that could explain entanglement without non-local influences. This led to significant developments in understanding quantum mechanics' foundations.

## Non-Locality
Quantum entanglement implies non-local correlations, meaning measurements on one particle can instantaneously affect another, regardless of distance. This phenomenon violates classical locality principles, suggesting a deeper connection between particles that defies intuitive classical physics.

## Key Concepts
- **Entanglement**: Particles share quantum states such that the state of one instantly influences the other.
- **Bell’s Theorem (1964)**: Provided a framework to test quantum mechanics against local hidden variable theories, with experiments confirming non-local correlations.
- **Decoherence**: The fragility of entanglement due to environmental interactions, crucial for developing practical quantum technologies.

## Applications
Quantum entanglement has diverse applications:
- **Quantum Cryptography**: Enables secure communication via Quantum Key Distribution (QKD), where eavesdropping disrupts entanglement.
- **Quantum Computing**: Uses entangled qubits for faster computation in specific tasks.
- **Quantum Teleportation**: Transfers quantum states over distances, enhancing communication and networking.
- **Quantum Sensors**: Develops advanced sensors for precise measurements in metrology, navigation, and imaging.

## Fundamental Physics
Experiments confirming Bell’s inequality violations have deepened our understanding of reality's fundamental principles. These findings support quantum mechanics' predictions about non-locality and challenge classical theories, shaping theoretical physics.

## Conclusion
Quantum entanglement is a cornerstone of modern quantum science, with profound implications for both theory and technology. Its exploration continues to expand our knowledge of the universe and opens new avenues in practical applications.
