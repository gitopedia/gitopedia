# Schrödinger's Cat

## Overview
Schrödinger's Cat is a thought experiment introduced by physicist Erwin Schrödinger in 1935. It illustrates quantum superposition, where a cat in a box is considered to be simultaneously alive and dead until the box is opened and observed. This paradox challenges classical notions of reality and highlights the peculiarities of quantum mechanics.

## History
The experiment was proposed by Schrödinger as a critique of the Copenhagen Interpretation, which suggests that a quantum system exists in multiple states until measured. Schrödinger's Cat serves to emphasize the strange implications of superposition on macroscopic objects.

## Key Concepts

### Superposition Principle
In quantum mechanics, particles like electrons can exist in multiple states simultaneously until observed. This principle applies to the cat, which is considered both alive and dead before measurement.

### Quantum States
Quantum systems are described by discrete states, such as spin up or down for an electron. These states represent possible outcomes upon observation.

### Copenhagen Interpretation
This interpretation posits that the wave function collapses into a single state when observed, determining the cat's fate upon opening the box.

### Many-Worlds Interpretation
An alternative view suggests that each possible outcome creates a parallel world. Thus, both the cat being alive and dead exist in separate universes until observation determines which one is reality.

## Interpretations

The thought experiment underscores the limitations of the Copenhagen Interpretation by highlighting observer-related issues. The Many-Worlds Interpretation offers a solution by proposing multiple parallel worlds, each reflecting a possible outcome of the experiment.
