# Double-Slit Experiment

The Double-Slit Experiment is a fundamental demonstration in quantum mechanics that illustrates the principles of wave-particle duality and the probabilistic nature of quantum systems.

## Overview
The experiment involves a barrier with two slits through which particles, such as electrons or photons, are directed. When particles pass through the slits, they create an interference pattern on a detection screen, similar to how light diffracts. This phenomenon was initially observed with light but has been extended to other quantum entities.

## History
The experiment was first described by Thomas Young in 1803 using light, demonstrating wave-like behavior. In the early 20th century, the experiment was adapted for electrons by Davisson and Germer, confirming that particles exhibit both particle and wave properties.

## Key Concepts
- **Wave-Particle Duality**: The experiment shows that particles can behave as waves, creating interference patterns when unobserved.
- **Quantum Superposition**: Particles exist in multiple states simultaneously until measured, which affects the observed pattern.
- **Observer Effect**: When particles are observed passing through the slits, they behave as particles, disrupting the interference pattern.

## Modern Applications
The Double-Slit Experiment has implications in quantum computing, cryptography, and understanding quantum mechanics' foundational principles. It challenges classical physics notions and supports the probabilistic interpretation of quantum theory.

This experiment remains a cornerstone in illustrating the strange and non-intuitive behavior of quantum systems.
