# Double-Slit Experiment

## Overview  
The double-slit experiment is a fundamental demonstration in quantum mechanics that reveals the wave-particle duality of matter and light. Originally conducted by Thomas Young in the early 19th century, it demonstrates how particles, such as photons or electrons, can exhibit both particle-like and wave-like properties depending on how they are observed.

## History  
The experiment was first performed by British scientist Thomas Young around 1801. It provided strong evidence for the wave theory of light, challenging Isaac Newton's corpuscular theory. Later developments in the 20th century extended the experiment to quantum particles, revealing profound insights into quantum mechanics.

## Key Concepts  
When particles are passed through a double slit, they create an interference pattern on a screen, characteristic of waves. However, when measured individually, each particle behaves like a discrete entity, akin to bullets passing through slits. This duality highlights the principles of superposition and entanglement in quantum mechanics.

## Quantum Mechanics Implications  
The experiment underscores the principle that particles exist in probabilistic states until observed or measured. This has led to significant advancements in understanding quantum phenomena, including encryption and computing technologies.

## Modern Variations  
Modern experiments have expanded upon Young's original setup, incorporating elements like quantum erasers and single-photon detectors to explore deeper aspects of quantum theory.

## Applications  
The double-slit experiment has influenced various fields, including technology development in areas such as quantum computing and communication. Its principles are essential for understanding and advancing these technologies.
