The Historical Roots of Existentialism: A Response to Modern Crises

Existentialism, often associated with thinkers like Jean-Paul Sartre, Søren Kierkegaard, and Friedrich Nietzsche, arose as a powerful philosophical movement that responds to the existential dilemmas faced by individuals in the modern world. Its roots can be traced to the profound shifts that took place in Europe during the 19th and 20th centuries, where religion’s traditional influence began to wane, and scientific advancements and industrialization altered the very fabric of human existence. Existentialism was not just an abstract theory but a response to an increasingly complex, uncertain, and fragmented world. This blog post delves into the historical background of existentialism, exploring how it emerged as a reaction to the crises of modernity, grappling with themes of nothingness, finitude, and the loss of a clear, divinely ordained purpose in life.

The decline of religious authority in the modern world 🔗

In the centuries leading up to the rise of existentialism, the traditional authority of religion was deeply entrenched in European society. Christianity, in particular, had long provided individuals with a framework for understanding the meaning of life, the nature of existence, and the purpose of suffering. Religious narratives offered answers to fundamental questions about life, death, and morality. However, the 19th and 20th centuries saw a dramatic decline in the influence of religion over people’s lives. The Enlightenment’s emphasis on reason and scientific inquiry began to challenge religious doctrines, leading to an increasing secularization of European society. At the same time, the scientific revolution, with figures like Charles Darwin and Galileo, shattered the medieval worldview that placed humanity at the center of the universe, offering instead a more impersonal and mechanistic vision of existence.

This decline in religious authority is often referred to by Friedrich Nietzsche as the “death of God.” Nietzsche famously proclaimed that “God is dead,” signaling the end of a divine moral order that had guided human behavior for centuries. For Nietzsche, this wasn’t just the loss of a religious figure but the collapse of an entire system of values and meaning. With God’s death came the recognition that humans were no longer provided with absolute truths or a transcendent purpose. This created a profound existential crisis, where individuals were left to confront the meaning of their existence without the comforting framework of religion. Nietzsche’s philosophy, particularly his concept of the Übermensch (Superman) and the eternal recurrence, reflects an effort to respond to this crisis by suggesting that individuals must create their own values and purpose in a world that no longer provides them.

The impact of scientific advancements and industrialization 🔗

In addition to the decline of religious authority, scientific advancements and the rapid industrialization of Europe played significant roles in shaping existential thought. The Industrial Revolution, which began in the late 18th century, dramatically changed the way people lived and worked. The rise of factories, urbanization, and the increasing mechanization of life led to a sense of alienation. People became cogs in the machinery of industry, and traditional ways of life were replaced by a more impersonal and fragmented social order.

Science also presented a new, more mechanistic view of the universe. Darwin’s theory of evolution and advances in physics, like those of Newton, offered explanations for natural phenomena that did not require divine intervention. These scientific breakthroughs, while groundbreaking, also contributed to a sense of disorientation for many individuals. The once-comforting idea that the universe had a purposeful design seemed to be replaced by an indifferent, vast cosmos operating according to natural laws. Humanity, once thought to be the centerpiece of God’s creation, was now seen as a product of blind evolution, subject to the forces of nature and chance. This view of existence, where meaning was not inherent in the world, gave rise to existential anxiety—the awareness that life may have no ultimate purpose or significance.

The crisis of nothingness and finitude 🔗

One of the central themes of existentialism is the experience of nothingness, which refers to the existential void that individuals face when they are confronted with the absence of inherent meaning or purpose in their lives. This sense of nothingness is not just a philosophical concept but an emotional and psychological experience. It is the feeling of being unmoored in a world where traditional sources of meaning—such as religion or societal norms—no longer provide answers. The existential void raises questions such as: What is the purpose of life? Why should we live? What happens after death? These questions, once answered by religious doctrines, now have no easy or comforting responses.

The experience of nothingness is closely tied to the concept of finitude—the realization that human life is finite, limited, and ultimately will come to an end. In the modern world, where scientific advances and medical breakthroughs have extended human life expectancy, the awareness of death has not been alleviated. Instead, it has been transformed into a more abstract concept. Individuals are forced to confront the reality that their existence is temporary and that everything they do will eventually be erased. This recognition can lead to feelings of despair and hopelessness, but existential thinkers argue that it also presents an opportunity for individuals to find their own meaning in life, rather than relying on external sources like religion or society.

The role of existential thinkers in articulating the crisis 🔗

Existentialist philosophers such as Søren Kierkegaard, Martin Heidegger, Jean-Paul Sartre, and Simone de Beauvoir were instrumental in articulating the existential crisis of nothingness and finitude. For Kierkegaard, the loss of religious certainty led to what he called the “sickness unto death”—the anxiety and despair that arise from confronting the possibility of a meaningless life. He argued that individuals must confront this despair head-on and make a “leap of faith” toward an authentic relationship with God, even in the face of uncertainty.

Martin Heidegger, another key existentialist, emphasized the concept of “Being-toward-death.” Heidegger suggested that the awareness of our own finitude is what gives life its meaning. He argued that most people live inauthentically, avoiding the reality of death and distracting themselves with trivial concerns. However, by fully embracing our mortality, Heidegger believed that we could live more authentically, making choices that reflect our true selves rather than living according to societal expectations or external pressures.

Jean-Paul Sartre, perhaps the most famous existentialist, took a radically different approach. For Sartre, existence precedes essence—meaning that humans are not born with a predefined purpose or nature. Instead, individuals are free to create their own meaning through their actions. This radical freedom can be both liberating and terrifying, as it places the burden of responsibility entirely on the individual. Sartre’s famous concept of “bad faith” describes the way in which people deceive themselves into thinking they are bound by external forces or roles, rather than accepting their freedom and responsibility to choose their own path. For Sartre, the awareness of nothingness and finitude is an opportunity for personal liberation, where individuals must embrace the void and take responsibility for their own existence.

The rise of existentialism as a cultural movement 🔗

Existentialism reached its peak in the 20th century, particularly after World War II. The horrors of the war, the rise of totalitarian regimes, and the atomization of society made existentialism a deeply relevant philosophical framework. The sense of alienation, despair, and hopelessness that had been explored by existential thinkers found a wide audience in a world that seemed increasingly unpredictable and chaotic.

In literature, existentialist themes appeared prominently in works by authors such as Albert Camus, Franz Kafka, and Samuel Beckett. Camus’s “The Stranger” and Kafka’s “The Trial” explore the absurdity of life and the alienation of the individual in a seemingly meaningless world. Samuel Beckett’s play “Waiting for Godot” captures the sense of waiting for something (or someone) that may never come, illustrating the absurdity and uncertainty of human existence.

In the realm of cinema, existentialism found expression in films such as Ingmar Bergman’s “The Seventh Seal” and Jean-Luc Godard’s “Alphaville.” These works, like the literature of the time, grappled with themes of death, existential crisis, and the search for meaning in a world that appeared indifferent to human suffering.

Existentialism emerged as a powerful response to the crises of modernity, where traditional sources of meaning—such as religion—began to lose their hold over individuals. The scientific advancements and industrialization of the time contributed to a growing sense of alienation, while the awareness of death and the experience of nothingness forced individuals to confront the absence of inherent meaning in life. Existentialist thinkers offered various responses to this crisis, emphasizing the importance of embracing freedom, responsibility, and the search for personal meaning. While existentialism may seem bleak or even nihilistic at first glance, it offers a path toward authenticity and self-discovery in a world that often seems indifferent or absurd.
