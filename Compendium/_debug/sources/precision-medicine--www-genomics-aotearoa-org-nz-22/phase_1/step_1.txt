**Precision Medicine (Personalized Medicine):**  
Precision medicine is an approach to healthcare that uses an individual's genetic information to guide medical decisions, tailoring treatments and preventative strategies to their unique genetic profile. Unlike traditional "one-size-fits-all" medicine, it focuses on understanding how a person's genes, environment, and lifestyle interact to influence their health and disease risk.  

**Key Aspects:**  
1. **Genetic Insights:**  
   - By sequencing a person’s genome (the complete set of genetic instructions), precision medicine identifies genetic variations that may contribute to disease, affect drug responses, or influence susceptibility to conditions like cancer, heart disease, or diabetes.  
   - For example, genetic testing can detect mutations in genes linked to hereditary cancers (e.g., BRCA1/BRCA2), enabling early interventions like preventative surgery.  

2. **Stratified Medicine:**  
   - This involves grouping patients based on shared genetic or biological traits to develop targeted treatments. It helps identify subgroups of people with distinct disease mechanisms or responses to therapies.  

3. **Applications:**  
   - **Diagnosis:** Identifying rare or undiagnosed diseases through genetic analysis.  
   - **Treatment:** Customizing drug therapies (e.g., pharmacogenomics) to improve efficacy and reduce side effects.  
   - **Prevention:** Assessing disease risk (e.g., heart disease) and recommending lifestyle or medical interventions.  
   - **Wellness:** Using genetic data to guide nutrition, exercise, and mental health strategies tailored to an individual’s profile.  

4. **Current Use Cases:**  
   - In oncology, genetic testing helps select therapies for cancers with known genetic drivers (e.g., targeted drugs for HER2-positive breast cancer).  
   - In pediatrics, it aids in diagnosing rare genetic disorders.  

**Challenges and Considerations:**  
- **Data Interpretation:** Not all genetic variations are fully understood, and results may not always provide clear answers for diagnosis or treatment.  
- **Population-Specific Data:** Precision medicine requires diverse genomic databases (e.g., for Māori, Polynesian, or non-European populations) to avoid disparities and ensure equitable care.  
- **Privacy and Ethics:** Secure storage of genetic data is critical, with informed consent and cultural respect (e.g., in New Zealand, Māori DNA is considered *taonga* and must be managed with guardianship principles).  

**Global Context:**  
- Initiatives like the UK’s 500,000 Genomes Project and the U.S. All of Us Research Program aim to expand genomic data for clinical use.  
- New Zealand is building a national genomic infrastructure to address health needs specific to its population, emphasizing Māori and Polynesian heritage.  

In essence, precision medicine represents a shift toward more individualized, effective, and proactive healthcare, driven by advances in genetics and data science.
