The History of Existentialism

Existentialism emerged as a significant philosophical movement during the 19th and 20th centuries, rooted in a response to historical and intellectual shifts that questioned human existence and the structures of traditional thought. To understand the origins and development of Existentialism, it is essential to examine the contexts and philosophical periods that shaped its rise.

The Context of Modern Philosophy and the Enlightenment

The Enlightenment, characterized by its emphasis on reason, science, and progress, laid much of the groundwork for modern philosophy. This period, spanning the 17th and 18th centuries, heralded a departure from medieval scholasticism and religious dogma, positioning human reason as the primary tool for unraveling life's mysteries. Thinkers such as René Descartes, John Locke, and Immanuel Kant emphasized autonomy, rationality, and systematic approaches to knowledge and morality. However, these optimistic visions of human reason also planted the seeds of doubt. By charting human existence through universal principles and systems, critics began to argue that such frameworks neglected individual experience and the complexities of human freedom.

This tension became more apparent as the Enlightenment gave rise to reactions both supportive of and critical toward its ideals. On one hand, the Industrial Revolution and scientific advancements appeared to validate the Enlightenment faith in progress. On the other hand, the increasing alienation, dehumanization, and disruptions brought by modernization spurred skepticism about whether this progress truly accounted for the lived experience of individuals. Within this setting, philosophy gradually turned toward personal existence and subjective inquiry as central themes.

German Idealism and Romanticism

The philosophy of German Idealism, particularly in the work of figures such as Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel, laid the groundwork for later existentialist concerns. Hegel's philosophy sought to reconcile the individual with the universal, exploring how consciousness and freedom unfold in history. However, the idealist emphasis on abstract, systematic approaches did not sit well with all thinkers. Søren Kierkegaard, often considered one of the forefathers of Existentialism, rejected Hegelian abstraction in favor of individual, passionate engagement with existence and faith.

Parallel to German Idealism, Romanticism emerged as a cultural and intellectual movement emphasizing emotion, imagination, and the individual's subjective experience. Romanticism celebrated the individual's ability to confront and create meaning in the face of nature, beauty, and even existential despair. These elements resonated deeply with existentialist themes, prioritizing personal experience over grand systems of thought.

Responses to Modernity in the 19th Century

The 19th century saw dramatic economic, social, and intellectual transformations driven by the Industrial Revolution, political upheavals, and scientific advancements. These shifts created a sense of dislocation, as traditional structures of religion, community, and meaning were increasingly called into question. Thinkers like Friedrich Nietzsche criticized the erosion of traditional values and the “death of God,” warning of the spiritual crises that could result from humanity's reliance on detached, rational systems.

Alongside Kierkegaard, Nietzsche would become central to Existentialism's development. Kierkegaard’s emphasis on the struggles of faith and individual responsibility and Nietzsche’s explorations of nihilism and the creation of values both opposed the dominant intellectual trends of their time. They laid the philosophical foundations for a focus on individual existence and meaning, which would be revisited by 20th-century existentialists.

The 20th Century and Existentialism's Flourishing

The early 20th century saw the waning of traditional philosophical systems and a growing interest in the more immediate concerns of human existence. This period was marked by profound upheavals, including two world wars, economic instability, and an accelerating march of technological change. Against this backdrop of uncertainty and crisis, Existentialism flourished as a response to the existential dilemmas of modernity. Thinkers such as Martin Heidegger, Jean-Paul Sartre, and Simone de Beauvoir further articulated the themes of existence, freedom, and responsibility emerging from the 19th-century intellectual context.

The world wars, in particular, shattered many longstanding beliefs about progress and the human condition. The mechanization of violence and the moral void left by widespread destruction led to a heightened focus on personal agency in an apparently chaotic and indifferent universe. Existentialism resonated with individuals grappling with these realities, venturing beyond academic philosophy to influence literature, art, and culture.

The Legacy of Existentialism

While Existentialism reached its peak in the mid-20th century, its historical trajectory reveals how it emerged from and engaged with major intellectual and social developments. Its roots lie in the Enlightenment’s rational optimism, the subjective turn of Romanticism, and the individualistic, existential critiques from Kierkegaard and Nietzsche. The backdrop of modernity’s crises—industrialization, war, and cultural displacement—offered fertile ground for a philosophy preoccupied with existence, freedom, and individual meaning.

Though subsequent philosophical movements, such as structuralism and poststructuralism, began to critique and move beyond Existentialism, its impact endures. The history of Existentialism reflects not only a specific philosophical tradition but also a broader inquiry into what it means to exist within a complex and often incomprehensible world.

Existentialism views the concept of the Other as a central element in understanding human existence and identity. The Other refers to another person or consciousness, which plays a role in shaping how an individual perceives themselves and their place in the world. According to existentialist thinkers, encountering the Other forces a person to recognize their subjectivity while also understanding that they are an object in the perception of someone else. This interaction creates a fundamental tension, as it highlights both the freedom and limitations inherent in human existence.

The presence of the Other challenges an individual’s autonomy, as it introduces the gaze or judgment of someone outside oneself. This can lead to feelings of vulnerability or discomfort, as people often struggle with the idea of being defined or viewed by someone else. However, this relationship also emphasizes the interconnectedness between individuals. Identity, in existentialist thought, is not formed in isolation but through relationships and interactions with others.

By acknowledging the Other, existentialism explores themes of self-awareness, freedom, and responsibility. It underscores how humans exist both as individuals and as beings in relation to others. This duality is essential to understanding existence and the complexities of human interaction. Ultimately, the Other plays a key role in existentialist philosophy by revealing the dynamics of self and otherness in the process of defining existence.

This example helps to demonstrate this philosophical perspective. Imagine a young artist who is passionately dedicated to her craft but begins to feel self-conscious after receiving critiques from others. She starts to question her own decisions, wondering if her art is truly authentic or if she is merely trying to conform to others' expectations. She becomes aware of how others perceive her, and this realization influences her actions and choices, making her feel that she is being defined by those outside of herself. The presence of "the Other" has prompted her to confront her freedom and responsibility in decision-making. While she seeks validation, she wrestles with the fear of losing her individuality. Her awareness of the opinions and judgments of others becomes a lens through which she evaluates her own existence, underscoring the tension between her internal sense of self and the external world.

Philosophers who object to or reject existentialism's view about the Other often do so because they find it incomplete or overly focused on individual subjectivity. One common criticism is that existentialism, by emphasizing personal freedom and responsibility, tends to understate the role of social structures, historical contexts, and cultural dynamics in shaping relationships with the Other. Critics argue that our engagements with others are not solely defined by individual choices or perceptions but are deeply influenced by external factors that existentialism overlooks.

Another objection arises from the notion that existentialism places too much weight on conflict or tension in relationships with Others. Some philosophers believe that this approach neglects the potential for genuine cooperation, empathy, and solidarity between individuals. They argue that relationships with the Other should not primarily be seen as sources of anxiety or challenges to one's freedom but as opportunities for mutual understanding and shared goals.

Additionally, critics sometimes take issue with what they see as existentialism's tendency to treat the Other as a somewhat abstract concept. They argue that this approach fails to capture the richness and diversity of real human relationships, which involve nuanced emotions, personal histories, and specific cultural or social contexts. For these critics, existentialism's view can feel detached from the lived realities of interacting with others.

Lastly, some philosophers reject existentialism's framework because they believe it conceptualizes human relationships in a way that is overly pessimistic or isolating. They may propose alternative frameworks, such as those found in ethics of care or dialogical philosophy, which emphasize connection, interdependence, and the intrinsic value of community. From their point of view, existentialism’s focus on individual self-definition seems to downplay the deep interconnectedness fundamental to human existence.

By bringing these objections to light, critics seek to address what they see as existentialism's limitations in understanding and exploring the complexities of human relationships with the Other.

These are some of the main reasons why grasping the concept of the Other is essential to comprehending existentialism.

Encourages Self-Awareness

The idea of the Other highlights the importance of self-awareness by emphasizing that humans do not exist in isolation. We define ourselves, in part, by recognizing how others perceive us. This concept helps individuals see themselves as part of a larger social context and provides insight into how their identity is shaped. The Other acts as a mirror, reflecting aspects of an individual’s character and behaviour that might otherwise go unnoticed. This awareness helps people better understand their own emotions, motivations, and actions, which can be empowering. It also invites individuals to consider how they influence those around them, fostering a broader understanding of their place in the world.

Highlights Human Connection

Recognizing the Other underscores the importance of relationships with others in human life. It shows how much of our existence revolves around interacting with other people—through communication, cooperation, and shared experiences. The presence of the Other reminds us that no one exists solely for themselves. Instead, life gains meaning through connections, relationships, and shared understanding. This perspective encourages empathy and compassion, as it prompts individuals to see the world from another person’s point of view. Acknowledging the Other brings greater appreciation for diversity in opinions, experiences, and cultures, enriching one’s worldview.

Promotes Ethical Responsibility

The idea of the Other often involves a sense of responsibility for how one’s actions impact others. When we acknowledge the Other, we also recognize their humanity and individuality. This recognition encourages ethical behaviour by reminding us that others have their own needs, desires, and vulnerabilities. It pushes individuals to consider how their choices affect not only themselves but also those around them. By focusing on the Other, people may develop a stronger sense of moral obligation and care toward others, encouraging fairness, mutual respect, and understanding in social interactions.

Existentialism’s view about the Other contrasts strongly with Hegel’s philosophy, particularly in how relationships and self-awareness are understood. For Hegel, the Other is central to achieving self-consciousness. He argues that individuals become aware of themselves through a dialectical relationship with others. This process, often described through the "master-slave dialectic," emphasizes interaction, recognition, and struggle for mutual acknowledgment as the pathway to self-realization.

Existentialism, on the other hand, often views the Other as a source of conflict or alienation rather than a path to self-awareness. For example, in Sartre's philosophy, the Other is seen as a threat to one's autonomy, capable of objectifying an individual through their gaze and imposing roles or expectations. This can lead to feelings of anxiety or a loss of freedom. Unlike Hegel’s mutual recognition, where the Other helps define the self in a cooperative dialectic, existentialism suggests a tension in which the Other might challenge one’s sense of individuality and freedom.

While both philosophies see the Other as significant, they fundamentally diverge in their interpretations. Hegel presents the Other as necessary for personal growth and interdependence, whereas existentialism often views the relationship as a complicated dynamic filled with potential for both understanding and existential struggle. This difference highlights existentialism’s focus on subjective experience and autonomy compared to Hegel’s emphasis on communal development and interconnectedness.

Meaning and Happiness

Existentialism views nothingness as a central concept that highlights the absence of inherent meaning in the universe. It suggests that individuals are confronted with a world that does not provide predetermined purpose or values. This lack of inherent structure emphasizes human freedom, as people are seen as entirely responsible for creating their own meaning and defining their existence. Nothingness is not just the absence of things but also represents the potential for change and the ability to redefine one’s life.

For existentialists, nothingness plays a critical role in understanding the human condition. It reveals the gaps or voids in life where no preexisting answers exist, forcing individuals to confront their own freedom and make authentic choices. This confrontation with nothingness can be unsettling, causing feelings of anxiety or dread. However, it also encourages individuals to come to terms with their responsibility for shaping their own existence.

Overall, in existentialism, nothingness does not merely signify emptiness but instead serves as an opening for human creativity and self-determination. By recognizing the absence of predetermined meaning, individuals are empowered to actively define their purpose and choose how they live, building their own sense of significance despite the void.

This example helps demonstrate this philosophical perspective. A scenario often discussed in existentialism is the feeling of nothingness that can arise when individuals question the meaning of their existence. Imagine someone working tirelessly in their career, only to one day realize they don’t find true fulfillment or purpose in what they do. They may feel a deep emptiness, as if their efforts and routines suddenly seem meaningless. This sense of nothingness might compel them to reevaluate their life choices and search for personal meaning beyond societal expectations or predefined roles. Some people may find this confrontation with nothingness unsettling, but existentialist thinkers often argue that this is an opportunity for growth and self-discovery. By confronting the lack of inherent meaning, individuals can take responsibility for creating their own purpose, shaping their lives in a way that reflects their personal values and desires.

Some philosophers object to existentialism’s focus on nothingness because they see it as overly negative or disconnected from a broader understanding of human life and values. One common criticism is that the concept of nothingness can be interpreted as dismissing the importance of traditional sources of meaning, such as religion, morality, or the pursuit of knowledge. Critics argue that existentialism, by emphasizing a void or lack of inherent meaning, might lead individuals to feelings of despair or nihilism rather than encouraging constructive ways of finding purpose.

Additionally, philosophers who champion rationalism or universal truths often reject existentialism’s reliance on subjective experiences. They believe that focusing on individual angst or personal freedom in the face of nothingness undervalues the shared structures that guide our lives, like reason, science, or ethical systems. For these thinkers, existentialism’s preoccupation with nothingness might appear as an overly solitary approach, limiting the potential for collective understanding and progress.

Another point of criticism comes from philosophers who value interconnectedness between individuals and the world. They might argue that existentialism’s view of nothingness isolates people from their environment and communities by placing too much emphasis on the internal struggle to create meaning. This, they believe, undermines the importance of relationships, traditions, and cultural foundations in helping shape our existence.

Finally, some critics find existentialism’s focus on nothingness to be unnecessarily abstract or vague. They believe philosophy should provide clarity and practical solutions to life’s big questions instead of dwelling on ideas that might be difficult for many to grasp or apply. For these philosophers, the idea of nothingness might seem like an intellectual dead end rather than a pathway to greater understanding or fulfillment. By focusing on more tangible or objective concepts, these critics argue that philosophy can better serve humanity’s needs.

Existentialism's concept of "nothingness" serves as a critical point of differentiation from Hegel's philosophy. For existentialists, nothingness is central to the human experience, highlighting the absence of intrinsic meaning or predetermined purpose in life. This absence creates a space for individuals to define their own essence through choices and actions, emphasizing personal freedom and responsibility. On the contrary, Hegel's philosophy revolves around the idea of a universal and rational process of development, where reality unfolds according to a dialectical progression towards absolute knowledge or spirit. For Hegel, everything, including contradictions, is ultimately synthesized into a higher, unified truth.

The key difference lies in how these philosophies treat the concept of meaning and unity. While existentialism centers on the subjective experience of individuals confronting a void or lack of inherent meaning, Hegel views reality as inherently structured and purposive, guided by a universal logic. Hegel's system leaves little room for the kind of radical freedom and confrontation with nothingness that existentialism emphasizes. Instead, Hegel focuses on reconciling opposites within a larger whole, suggesting that even struggles and negations have their place in an overarching, meaningful order.

Ultimately, existentialism views nothingness as a challenge and an opportunity to assert individual autonomy, whereas Hegel's philosophy seeks to dissolve the appearance of nothingness into a greater synthesis within a universal, evolving system of meaning.

Alienation, in this context, reflects the tension between the individual’s desire for purpose and the absence of intrinsic meaning in life.

Alienation, therefore, is treated not as something external imposed by society or circumstances but as an inherent part of human freedom and individuality, closely tied to the existential quest for self-understanding and purpose.

Some philosophers object to Existentialism’s views about alienation because they believe it focuses too heavily on individual experience, neglecting broader social and cultural factors. For example, Marxist philosophers see alienation as stemming from capitalism, where workers feel disconnected from the products of their labour because they have no control over the production process. From this perspective, existentialism falls short by emphasizing an individual's subjective struggle rather than addressing the need for societal change to eliminate alienation on a larger scale.

Another objection comes from philosophers who question the emphasis existentialism places on freedom and choice. Some suggest that this view underestimates the extent to which external circumstances, such as upbringing, culture, or economic conditions, limit people’s ability to truly choose their way of life. Critics argue that alienation cannot be resolved solely through individual introspection or decision-making, as existentialism often suggests. Instead, they believe that addressing alienation requires collective action to improve the social conditions that restrict individual freedoms.

Additionally, some philosophers take issue with existentialism’s rather bleak or pessimistic tone regarding human existence. They argue that by framing alienation as an almost inevitable part of life, existentialism risks encouraging a sense of despair or resignation instead of offering a practical solution to overcome the issue. Critics from humanistic traditions, for instance, believe in focusing on the potential for human growth and connection, emphasizing understanding and empathy as pathways to reduce feelings of alienation.

Overall, these objections reflect a concern that existentialism’s view of alienation may be overly narrow or abstract, failing to account for the broader social, cultural, and systemic forces that influence human experience. By addressing these critiques, we gain a more comprehensive perspective on the complex phenomenon of alienation.

Existentialism and Stoic philosophy offer distinct perspectives on the concept of despair, highlighting both similarities and differences in their approach. While both philosophies acknowledge the existence of despair, they diverge in their understanding and response to it.

Existentialism, rooted in the belief that individuals have the freedom to create their own meaning in life, recognizes despair as an inherent aspect of human existence. Existentialists view despair as a natural response to the existential crisis of confronting the inherent absurdity and uncertainties of life. They emphasize the importance of acknowledging and confronting despair as a means of personal growth and self-discovery. Rather than avoiding or suppressing despair, existentialists encourage individuals to embrace it as an opportunity for authentic personal transformation and the creation of meaning.

On the other hand, Stoic philosophy approaches despair from a different angle. Stoics believe in accepting the natural order of the universe, emphasizing the importance of living in accordance with reason and virtue. They view despair as a result of misguided desires and attachments to external circumstances beyond our control. Stoics advocate for cultivating inner resilience, finding tranquility, and maintaining a sense of inner peace despite external circumstances. Rather than dwelling on despair, Stoicism encourages individuals to focus on what they can control and develop a sense of equanimity in the face of adversity.

In summary, while both existentialism and Stoic philosophy recognize the existence of despair, they differ in their approach. Existentialism emphasizes confronting despair as a means of personal growth and creating meaning, while Stoicism focuses on accepting the natural order of the universe and cultivating inner resilience. Both philosophies offer valuable insights and strategies for navigating the complexities of human existence and finding meaning in the face of despair.

Angst arises from the realization that humans are inherently free and responsible for their own actions and choices. This freedom brings with it a sense of burden and a fear of making wrong decisions or failing to live up to one's own potential. Angst can be triggered by contemplating the meaning and purpose of life, the inevitability of death, and the absence of any predetermined or objective values or truths.

Existentialism posits that embracing and confronting angst can lead to personal growth and self-discovery. By acknowledging the anxieties and uncertainties that come with existence, individuals have the opportunity to confront the challenges of life head-on and make authentic choices that align with their own values and beliefs. While angst can be uncomfortable and unsettling, it is seen as an essential part of the human experience, providing the impetus for individuals to search for meaning and create their own sense of purpose in an otherwise chaotic and unpredictable world.

In essence, existentialism's view of angst is that it is an inherent aspect of human existence, stemming from the inherent freedom and responsibility that individuals possess. By embracing angst and confronting the uncertainties of life, individuals have the opportunity to shape their own paths and find meaning in the midst of existential questions.

Reasons why some philosophers object to or reject existentialism's view about angst can vary based on their own philosophical perspectives and beliefs. Here are a few commonly raised objections:

Subjective Nature of Angst: Critics argue that existentialism's view of angst may overly focus on individual subjectivity, neglecting the role of external factors and social influences. They contend that angst is not solely a personal existential experience but is also shaped by societal, cultural, and historical contexts.

Denial of Objective Truth: Some philosophers object to existentialism's emphasis on subjective truth and the rejection of objective truths. They argue that existentialism's view of angst as an inherent part of the human condition may downplay the significance of seeking objective meaning and truth in life.

Reliance on Individual Autonomy: Critics suggest that existentialism's focus on individual autonomy and freedom may lead to a self-centered perspective that overlooks the importance of communal values, social responsibilities, and ethical considerations. They contend that existentialism's view of angst may promote a self-centeredness that undermines collective well-being and moral obligations.

Potential for Nihilism: Some philosophers object to existentialism's view of angst, expressing concerns that it may lead to a sense of nihilism or despair. They argue that the emphasis on the absurdity and meaninglessness of existence, without providing a clear framework for finding purpose or moral guidance, can result in existential nihilism.

Neglect of Human Interconnectedness: Critics argue that existentialism's view of angst may not adequately account for the fundamental interconnectedness of human existence. They suggest that the shared experiences, relationships, and social bonds that shape individuals' lives should be considered alongside personal angst, as they play a significant role in shaping one's identity and experiences of meaning.

It is important to note that these objections do not dismiss existentialism in its entirety but rather offer alternative perspectives and critiques. Philosophical debates and disagreements are essential for further exploration and understanding of complex concepts like angst and existentialism, contributing to the richness and diversity of philosophical discourse.

Existentialism views despair of finitude as a condition that arises when individuals are overly attached to worldly things or external factors, such as possessions, relationships, or societal roles. These attachments define their sense of self and purpose, often limiting them to a narrow understanding of existence. According to existentialist thought, this form of despair occurs because people place their identity and meaning in something finite and ultimately transient. Over time, they may realize that these external factors are fragile and incapable of providing lasting fulfillment or a true sense of identity.

This realization leads to an inner conflict, as individuals struggle with the tension between their dependence on finite things and their deeper yearning for meaning beyond those limitations. Existentialism emphasizes that this despair stems from a fundamental disconnect between the individual's potential for freedom and the attempt to ground their existence in something that cannot endure indefinitely. By clinging to what is finite, they fail to fully grasp the broader possibilities of existence, which creates a deep sense of dissatisfaction and unease.

This example helps demonstrate this philosophical perspective. Imagine an individual so consumed by their pursuit of wealth and success that they ignore the limited nature of their time on earth. They invest all their identity and purpose into external achievements, believing these accomplishments will define their worth. However, as life progresses, they are confronted by the realization that wealth and power cannot shield them from mortality or provide true, lasting fulfillment. This realization leads to a profound sense of despair, as the individual grapples with the overwhelming gulf between their external identity and internal existence. The despair grows as they recognize the vast disconnect between attempting to find meaning in finite, transient things and the void of deeper personal understanding. This confrontation with the limits of existence underscores the painful turmoil of living without fully integrating one’s finite reality into a broader sense of purpose.

Some philosophers object to or reject existentialism's perspective because they view despair differently or prioritize other aspects of human experience. One key criticism comes from thinkers who believe existentialism overemphasizes individual subjectivity and emotions like despair, making it less grounded in a shared or collective understanding of human life. These critics argue that focusing too much on subjective experience can lead to a distorted or incomplete view of human existence, potentially neglecting societal, cultural, or relational factors that also shape who we are.

Others reject existentialism's approach because they see despair as neither universal nor as central to understanding the human condition. Philosophers from traditions like stoicism, for instance, might argue that despair reflects a failure of perspective or self-discipline, not a fundamental truth about life. To them, emotions such as despair are manageable through reason and reflection, and should not define how we approach existence. Instead of seeing despair as inevitable, they advocate for cultivating habits of resilience, finding peace through acceptance of external realities, and focusing on personal growth.

Additionally, some religious philosophers oppose existentialist ideas for their perceived lack of spiritual grounding. From their viewpoint, despair stems not from human finitude but from a disconnection from a higher power or divine reality. They believe that existentialism fails to acknowledge the role of faith and divine purpose in resolving despair. These thinkers often assert that hope, rooted in religious belief, offers a more satisfying or complete response to life’s challenges than existentialism's focus on self-defined meaning in the face of despair.

Finally, others criticize existentialism for underestimating the role of community or external support systems in overcoming despair. They argue that humans are inherently social beings and that despair can often be alleviated through relationships, shared purpose, and collective efforts. According to these critics, existentialism tends to isolate the individual, placing all responsibility for meaning and resolution on a single person, which can be an overwhelming and unrealistic burden.

These objections highlight diverse perspectives on despair, reflecting broader philosophical debates about the nature of human existence and the paths to a meaningful life.
