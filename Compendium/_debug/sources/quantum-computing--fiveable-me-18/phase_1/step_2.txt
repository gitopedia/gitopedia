- 1.1 Historical context and motivation for quantum computing

- Quantum computing emerged in the 1980s, blending quantum mechanics with computer science. This revolutionary field promises to solve complex problems faster than classical computers, sparking excitement in cryptography, drug discovery, and optimization.

- From early theoretical concepts to recent demonstrations of quantum supremacy, the field has rapidly evolved. Major tech companies and research institutions continue pushing boundaries, aiming to harness quantum computing's potential for groundbreaking applications across various industries.

- Motivations for quantum technology

- Limitations of classical computing Moore's Law: The observation that the number of transistors in a dense integrated circuit doubles about every two years, leading to concerns about the eventual limits of classical computing as transistors approach the size of individual atoms Certain problems, such as factoring large numbers (cryptography) and simulating complex quantum systems (drug discovery), are difficult or impossible for classical computers to solve efficiently due to the exponential growth of computational resources required

- Moore's Law: The observation that the number of transistors in a dense integrated circuit doubles about every two years, leading to concerns about the eventual limits of classical computing as transistors approach the size of individual atoms

- Certain problems, such as factoring large numbers (cryptography) and simulating complex quantum systems (drug discovery), are difficult or impossible for classical computers to solve efficiently due to the exponential growth of computational resources required

- Potential for quantum speedup Quantum parallelism: The ability of quantum computers to perform many calculations simultaneously by leveraging superposition, enabling them to explore multiple solutions in parallel Exponential speedup: Quantum algorithms, such as Shor's algorithm for factoring and Grover's algorithm for searching, can solve certain problems exponentially faster than the best known classical algorithms

- Quantum parallelism: The ability of quantum computers to perform many calculations simultaneously by leveraging superposition, enabling them to explore multiple solutions in parallel

- Exponential speedup: Quantum algorithms, such as Shor's algorithm for factoring and Grover's algorithm for searching, can solve certain problems exponentially faster than the best known classical algorithms

- Applications of quantum computing Cryptography: Quantum computers could break current public-key cryptography systems (RSA), necessitating the development of quantum-resistant cryptography to ensure secure communication and transactions Drug discovery and materials science: Quantum computers could efficiently simulate molecular interactions, leading to breakthroughs in the discovery of new drugs and materials with desired properties Optimization problems: Quantum algorithms could be used to solve complex optimization problems in logistics (vehicle routing), finance (portfolio optimization), and machine learning (training neural networks)

- Cryptography: Quantum computers could break current public-key cryptography systems (RSA), necessitating the development of quantum-resistant cryptography to ensure secure communication and transactions

- Drug discovery and materials science: Quantum computers could efficiently simulate molecular interactions, leading to breakthroughs in the discovery of new drugs and materials with desired properties

- Optimization problems: Quantum algorithms could be used to solve complex optimization problems in logistics (vehicle routing), finance (portfolio optimization), and machine learning (training neural networks)

- Milestones in quantum computing development

- Theoretical foundations 1980s: David Deutsch (University of Oxford) proposed the concept of a universal quantum computer and described the first quantum algorithm, laying the theoretical groundwork for the field 1994: Peter Shor (AT&T Bell Labs) developed Shor's algorithm for factoring large numbers, demonstrating the potential for quantum computers to solve problems intractable for classical computers 1996: Lov Grover (Bell Labs) introduced Grover's algorithm for searching unstructured databases, providing a quadratic speedup over classical search algorithms

- 1980s: David Deutsch (University of Oxford) proposed the concept of a universal quantum computer and described the first quantum algorithm, laying the theoretical groundwork for the field

- 1994: Peter Shor (AT&T Bell Labs) developed Shor's algorithm for factoring large numbers, demonstrating the potential for quantum computers to solve problems intractable for classical computers

- 1996: Lov Grover (Bell Labs) introduced Grover's algorithm for searching unstructured databases, providing a quadratic speedup over classical search algorithms

- Experimental realizations 1998: Isaac Chuang (IBM) and Neil Gershenfeld (MIT) demonstrated the first quantum algorithm on a 2-qubit NMR quantum computer, proving the feasibility of building working quantum computers 1999: D-Wave Systems, founded by Geordie Rose and Haig Farris, sold the first quantum computing system, marking the beginning of commercial quantum computing 2016: IBM made the first quantum computer available on the cloud, enabling researchers and developers to access quantum hardware remotely and explore practical applications

- 1998: Isaac Chuang (IBM) and Neil Gershenfeld (MIT) demonstrated the first quantum algorithm on a 2-qubit NMR quantum computer, proving the feasibility of building working quantum computers

- 1999: D-Wave Systems, founded by Geordie Rose and Haig Farris, sold the first quantum computing system, marking the beginning of commercial quantum computing

- 2016: IBM made the first quantum computer available on the cloud, enabling researchers and developers to access quantum hardware remotely and explore practical applications

- Recent advancements 2019: Google, in collaboration with NASA and Oak Ridge National Laboratory, claimed to have achieved quantum supremacy with a 53-qubit processor called Sycamore, performing a specific task in 200 seconds that would take a classical supercomputer 10,000 years 2020: China's University of Science and Technology demonstrated quantum supremacy with their Jiuzhang quantum computer, performing Gaussian boson sampling in 200 seconds, a task that would take classical supercomputers 600 million years Ongoing research and development by major tech companies (Google, IBM, Microsoft, Intel) and research institutions (MIT, Caltech, University of Oxford) to improve quantum hardware and software, increasing qubit counts, reducing error rates, and developing practical applications
