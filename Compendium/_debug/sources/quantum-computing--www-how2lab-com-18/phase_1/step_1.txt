**History of Quantum Computing**  
**Key Figures and Milestones**  
- **Richard Feynman (1982):** Proposed quantum computing as a tool to simulate quantum systems, sparking foundational research.  
- **David Deutsch (1985):** Formalized the concept of a universal quantum computer, introducing quantum algorithms.  
- **Peter Shor (1994):** Developed Shor’s algorithm for factoring large numbers, demonstrating quantum computing’s potential for cryptography.  
- **Lov Grover (1996):** Introduced Grover’s algorithm for unstructured search, highlighting quantum speedup.  

**Qubit Technologies**  
- **Superconducting Qubits:** Used by IBM and Google, relying on Josephson junctions and cryogenic cooling.  
- **Trapped-Ion Qubits:** Utilized by companies like Quantinuum, leveraging laser control for high-fidelity operations.  
- **Photonic Qubits:** Employed in systems like China’s Jiuzhang photonic quantum computer for boson sampling.  

**Government and Industry Initiatives**  
- **U.S. National Quantum Initiative (2024):** Allocates billions for quantum research, with IBM and Google as key players.  
- **China’s Quantum Program:** Achievements like Jiuzhang 3.0 and investments in quantum communication and computation.  
- **EU Quantum Flagship:** Focuses on hybrid quantum-classical algorithms and industrial applications.  

**Challenges and Future Directions**  
- **Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) Devices:** Current systems face decoherence, limited qubit counts, and high error rates.  
- **Error Correction:** Requires thousands to millions of physical qubits to encode a single logical qubit, a hurdle for scalability.  
- **Hybrid Algorithms:** Combining quantum and classical resources for near-term applications in optimization, cryptography, and materials science.  
- **Fault-Tolerant Systems:** Long-term goals include error-corrected, scalable quantum computers for reliable large-scale computation.

**Quantum vs. Classical Computing**  
- **Classical Computing:** Relies on bits (0/1) and deterministic operations.  
- **Quantum Computing:** Uses qubits, enabling superposition (0/1 simultaneously) and entanglement for parallel processing.  

**Power of Qubits: Superposition and Entanglement**  
- **Superposition:** Qubits can exist in multiple states at once, enabling exponential computational power.  
- **Entanglement:** Correlates qubits across distances, allowing instantaneous state synchronization for complex calculations.
