# Largest-ever ‘superposition’ supersizes Schrödinger’s cat
- Physicists have created the largest ever 'superposition' with a cluster of around 7,000 atoms of sodium metal, each spaced 133 nanometres apart.
- The experiment demonstrates that these clusters can behave like waves, spreading out into spatially distinct paths and interfering to form detectable patterns.
- This result was published in Nature on 21 January.

# Quantum Superposition Experiment Details
- The experiment was conducted by a team based at the University of Vienna, led by Sebastian Pedalino and Stefan Gerlich.
- The clusters were generated as a beam at 77 degrees kelvin (−196 ºC) in an ultra-high vacuum.
- The setup involved an interferometer with three gratings constructed using laser beams to channel the clusters through narrow gaps and measure their interference patterns.

# Challenges in the Experiment
- Stray gas molecules, light, or electric fields can disrupt the quantum state, making it difficult to observe the superposition.
- It took two years for the team to detect a signal due to these challenges.
- Co-author Stefan Gerlich noted that this experiment was previously considered "not possible" 15 years ago.

# Significance of the Experiment
- The experiment is ten times bigger than the previous record in terms of macroscopicity, which combines mass, duration of the quantum state, and separation of states.
- While it's not the largest mass ever put into a superposition (a 16-microgram vibrating crystal was used in 2023), this experiment represents a significant advancement.
- The team is also exploring experiments with biological matter, such as viruses, which are similar in size to the clusters.

# Philosophical and Practical Implications
- This experiment addresses the question of whether there's a transition between quantum and classical states, contributing to the understanding of how the everyday world emerges from quantum mechanics.
- Quantum computers will need to maintain large numbers of objects in superpositions for useful calculations, making this research relevant to future technologies.

# Future Directions
- Scaling up further is challenging due to shorter wavelengths of more massive particles, which make it harder to distinguish quantum predictions from classical ones.
- The team aims to continue pushing the boundaries of quantum experiments, potentially including biological matter.

# Expert Opinions
- Sandra Eibenberger-Arias of the Fritz Haber Institute described the result as "fantastic" and emphasized its importance in understanding the transition between quantum and classical states.
- Giulia Rubino of the University of Bristol highlighted the practical implications for quantum computing and the need to ensure that quantum systems do not collapse prematurely.
