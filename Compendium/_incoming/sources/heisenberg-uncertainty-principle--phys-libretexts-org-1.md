# Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle

- The Heisenberg uncertainty principle is a key principle in quantum mechanics.
- It states that if the uncertainty of position (Δx) is small, the uncertainty of momentum (Δp) must be large, and vice versa.
- Versions of the uncertainty principle also exist for other quantities such as energy and time.

## Momentum and Position

- The momentum-position uncertainty principle can be expressed as:
  \[
  Δ⁢x⁢Δ⁢p≥ℏ2
  \]
- For a free particle moving along the x-direction, the wavefunction is uniform and independent of time.
- A localized particle is modeled by a linear superposition of free-particle states called a wave packet.
- The minimum uncertainty product for a Gaussian function (bell-curve distribution) gives:
  - For an electron: Δ⁢p = \(9.1×10^{-34} \, kg⋅m/s\), Δ⁢x = \(5.8 \, cm\).
  - For a bowling ball: Δ⁢p = \(6.0×10^{-3} \, kg⋅m/s\), Δ⁢x = \(8.8×10^{-33} \, m\).

## Energy and Time

- The energy-time uncertainty principle is expressed as:
  \[
  Δ⁢E⁢Δ⁢t≥ℏ2
  \]
- A quantum state that exists for only a short time cannot have a definite energy.
- Finite lifetimes of excited states in atoms result in spectral line widths.
- Example calculations:
  - For an atom with Δ⁢t = \(10^{-8} \, s\) and average frequency \(f = 7.1×10^{14} \, Hz\):
    - Δ⁢f ≈ \(8.0×10^{6} \, Hz\).
    - Δ⁢ff ≈ \(1.1×10^{-8}\), indicating monochromatic emission.
  - For a sodium atom with lifetime \(Δ⁢t = 1.6×10^{-8} \, s\) and photon energy \(E = 2.105 \, eV\):
    - Δ⁢E ≈ \(4.1×10^{-8} \, eV\).
    - Spectral line width ≈ \(1.1×10^{-5} \, nm\).

## Ground-State Energy of a Hydrogen Atom

- The ground-state energy can be estimated using the uncertainty principle.
- For an electron in a hydrogen atom modeled as a particle in a one-dimensional box:
  - Length \(L = 0.1 \, nm\).
  - Ground-state energy \(E_0 ≈ 1 \, eV\).

## General Meaning

- The uncertainty principle does not depend on the precision of experimental apparatus.
- It originates from the wave-like nature of matter and imposes limits on simultaneous measurements of position and momentum.

## Applications

- Used to estimate uncertainties in quantum states and spectral line widths.
- Confirms that macroscopic systems exhibit negligible effects due to the small value of Planck’s constant.
