**Schrödinger's Cat**

**Overview**  
Schrödinger's Cat is a thought experiment introduced by physicist Erwin Schrödinger in 1935 to illustrate challenges in quantum mechanics, particularly the concept of superposition and measurement.

**Historical Context**  
Developed by Schrödinger to counter the Copenhagen Interpretation of quantum mechanics, which posits that particles exist in multiple states until measured. Schrödinger aimed to highlight issues with applying quantum principles to macroscopic objects.

**Key Concepts**  
- **Superposition**: A quantum system can exist in multiple states simultaneously.
- **Wave Function**: Mathematically describes the probabilities of a particle's state, collapsing upon measurement.

**The Thought Experiment**  
A cat is placed in a box with a radioactive atom. If the atom decays, it releases poison, killing the cat. A Geiger counter detects decay; if triggered, it opens a valve releasing poison. Until observed, the wave function suggests the cat is both alive and dead, illustrating quantum superposition.

**Interpretations**  
- **Copenhagen Interpretation**: Observation collapses the wavefunction into one state.
- **Many Worlds Interpretation**: Each possible state exists in separate universes.

**Criticisms and Limitations**  
The experiment is more theoretical than practical. Macroscopic objects interact with environments, causing numerous hidden measurements, which precludes true superposition. Thus, it primarily serves as a discussion tool rather than a literal scenario.

**Significance**  
Despite its limitations, Schrödinger's Cat remains a pivotal concept in quantum discussions, emphasizing the oddities of quantum mechanics and sparking debates on interpretation.
