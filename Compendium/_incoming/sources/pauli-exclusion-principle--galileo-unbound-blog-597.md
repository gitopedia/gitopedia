# Introduction
- Wolfgang Pauli submitted a paper to Zeitschrift für Physik in December 1924 that connected Bohr’s model of the atom to the structure of the periodic table.
- This work introduced a new quantum number into physics, which governs how matter organizes itself.

# Background on Pauli's Early Work
- Pauli was reading Einstein on relativity and Emil Jordan on functional analysis before starting his university studies in Munich with Arnold Sommerfeld.
- Felix Klein approached Sommerfeld to write an article on relativity theory for his Encyclopedia of Mathematical Sciences. Sommerfeld suggested Pauli, who was still a student.
- Pauli’s 250-page encyclopedia article on relativity was published in 1921 when he was only 21 years old.

# The Anomalous Zeeman Effect
- In 1922, Pauli began studying the anomalous Zeeman effect, which involved the splitting of optical transitions in atoms under magnetic fields.
- The Bohr model had quantized angular momentum into integer values, but the anomalous Zeeman effect showed splittings that could only be explained with half-integers.

# The Periodic Table and Quantum Numbers
- The periodic table’s structure was based on a sequence of differences of squares: 2, 8, 18.
- Sommerfeld referred to this number sequence as “cabalistic.”

# Bohr's Model and the Core Problem
- Bohr’s theory explained energy levels in hydrogen but struggled with how electrons filled different levels in more complex atoms.
- The noble gas core played a role in Bohr’s model, contributing to both normal and anomalous Zeeman effects.

# Pauli's Relativistic Effects Calculation
- Pauli calculated relativistic effects on the Zeeman splittings for high-Z atoms but found no relativistic corrections for Thallium (Z = 90).
- This led him to reject the existing core model of angular momentum and focus on the outer valence electron.

# Pauli's Breakthrough and Exclusion Principle
- By November 1924, Pauli concluded that electrons could exist in two states with different angular momenta.
- He introduced a new fourth quantum number (m2) and proposed that no two electrons could have the same set of quantum numbers.
- This became known as the Pauli Exclusion Principle.

# Connection to Electron Spin
- Pauli’s concept of Zweideutigkeit was later identified as electron spin by Ralph Kronig, George Uhlenbeck, and Samuel Goudsmit in 1925.
- The antisymmetry of electron wavefunctions, described by Dirac in 1926, provided a mathematical foundation for the Exclusion Principle.

# Historical Timeline
- 1845: Faraday effect (rotation of light polarization in a magnetic field)
- 1896: Zeeman effect (splitting of optical transitions in a magnetic field)
- 1897: Anomalous Zeeman effect (half-integer splittings)
- 1902: Lorentz and Zeeman awarded Nobel Prize
- 1921: Paschen-Back effect (strong-field Zeeman effect)
- 1922: Stern-Gerlach experiment (space quantization)
- 1924: de Broglie matter waves, Bose statistics of photons, Stoner’s work on state conservation, and Pauli’s Exclusion Principle
