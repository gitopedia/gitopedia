# Indus Valley Civilization

## Historical Context
The Indus Valley Civilization, also known as the Harappan Civilization, was a Bronze Age society that flourished in the region encompassing modern-day northeast Afghanistan, Pakistan, and northwest India. It is considered one of the three cradles of civilization in the ancient world, alongside Ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia. The civilization's lifespan is typically divided into three phases: the Early Harappan Phase (3300–2600 BCE), the Mature Harappan Phase (2600–1900 BCE), and the Late Harappan Phase (1900–1300 BCE).

The civilization's significance lies in its advanced urban planning, sophisticated drainage systems, and the development of new techniques in metallurgy and handicraft. Its discovery in the early 20th century by British archaeologists provided critical insights into the ancient cultures of the Indian subcontinent.

## Key Phases of the Civilization
- The Early Harappan Phase (3300–2600 BCE) marked the initial development of the civilization, characterized by the emergence of small settlements and the beginnings of agricultural practices.
- The Mature Harappan Phase (2600–1900 BCE) represents the civilization's peak, with the establishment of major cities like Harappa and Mohenjo-daro. This period saw the refinement of urban planning, standardized weights and measures, and the widespread use of seals for trade and administrative purposes.
- The Late Harappan Phase (1900–1300 BCE) is associated with the gradual decline of the civilization, possibly due to environmental changes, shifting trade routes, or internal factors. By 1300 BCE, the civilization had largely disappeared, though some smaller settlements persisted.

## Archaeological Discoveries
- The discovery of Harappa in 1921–1922 by Sir John Hubert Marshall marked the beginning of systematic excavations of the Indus Valley Civilization. Marshall's work led to the identification of Harappa as a major urban center.
- Mohenjo-daro, another key site, was largely excavated by Marshall and Sir Mortimer Wheeler by 1931. By 1999, over 1,056 cities and settlements of the Indus Civilization had been identified, with 96 of them excavated.
- The ruins of Harappa were first described in 1842 by Charles Masson, who noted the presence of well-burnt bricks. These bricks were later used as ballast for the East Indian Railway Company's track, inadvertently damaging the site.
- The discovery of Harappan seals in 1912 by John Faithfull Fleet prompted further excavations, leading to the formal study of the civilization.

## Cities of the Indus Valley Civilization
### Harappa
- Harappa was a fortified city located in modern-day Pakistan, believed to have housed up to 23,500 residents. The city covered an area of 150 hectares (370 acres) and featured administrative and religious centers.
- The modern village of Harappa, used as a railway station during British rule, is six kilometers from the ancient site, which suffered damage during the British period.
- The city's architecture included sculpted houses with flat roofs made of red sand and clay.
