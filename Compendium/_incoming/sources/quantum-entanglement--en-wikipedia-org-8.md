**Quantum Entanglement: An Encyclopedia Entry**

**Definition and Historical Context**
Quantum entanglement is a phenomenon where pairs or groups of particles interact such that the quantum state of each particle cannot be described independently of the others, even when separated by large distances. This connection was first articulated by Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen (EPR) in 1935, who questioned its implications for locality. John Bell later demonstrated through his theorem that quantum mechanics defies local hidden variables, proving entanglement's non-local nature.

**Properties of Entangled States**
Entangled states, such as EPR pairs, singlet states, triplet states, and Greenberger–Horne–Zeilinger (GHZ) states, exhibit correlations stronger than classical physics allows. Measuring one particle instantaneously influences the state of another, regardless of distance, a property known as non-locality.

**Applications**
- **Quantum Computing:** Entanglement is essential for quantum bits (qubits), enabling superior computational power.
- **Quantum Communication:** Used in protocols like quantum key distribution for secure communication.
- **Quantum Sensing and Metrology:** Enhances measurement precision through entangled states.
- **Quantum Teleportation:** Facilitates the transfer of quantum states over distances.

**Challenges**
Decoherence, caused by environmental interactions, disrupts entanglement. Scaling entangled systems for practical applications remains a significant challenge.

**Experimental Verification**
Bell tests have confirmed entanglement's existence, with notable experiments like those by Yin et al. achieving long-distance entanglement (1,203 km). Recent studies include entangling macroscopic objects and high-energy particles, such as top quarks and mechanical oscillators.

**Broader Implications**
Entanglement challenges our understanding of spacetime and gravity, suggesting that spacetime may emerge from quantum entanglement. It also bridges quantum mechanics with fields like quantum gravity and high-energy physics.

In summary, quantum entanglement is a fundamental aspect of quantum mechanics with profound implications for both theoretical physics and technological applications, continuing to inspire advancements in our understanding of the universe and technology development.
