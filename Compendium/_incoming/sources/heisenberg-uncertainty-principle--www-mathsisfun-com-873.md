# Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle
- The principle states that the better we know a quantum particle's position, the less well we know its momentum, and vice versa.
- The formula is given by σx * σp ≥ h4π, where σx is the uncertainty in position, σp is the uncertainty in momentum, and h is Planck's constant (6.626 070 15 × 10−34 Js).
- If σx is small, σp must be large, and if σx is large, σp must be small.
- The principle arises due to the wave-particle duality of quantum particles.
- The de Broglie equation links wavelength (λ) and momentum (p): λ = h/p.
- A sine wave demonstrates that knowing its wavelength perfectly makes its position uncertain, and vice versa.
- Combining multiple cosine waves results in a well-defined position but with less certainty about the wavelength.
- Fourier series can be used to create complex wave patterns, illustrating the trade-off between position and wavelength certainty.
- In classical physics, the uncertainty is negligible for large objects like billiard balls or planets.
- ħ (reduced Planck's constant) is equal to h/(2π).
- The principle also applies to energy (σE) and lifetime (σt) of particles: σE * σt ≥ ħ/2.
- Short-lived particles have less defined energy, while long-lived particles have more defined energy.
- Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle is essential to quantum mechanics, explaining phenomena like electron clouds in atoms.
- The principle underpins technologies such as quantum computing and advanced microscopes.

# Photons Through a Slit
- When a laser beam passes through a thin slit, it creates an interference pattern on the wall due to the wave-like nature of photons (and electrons).
- This phenomenon demonstrates another aspect of Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle.
- Unlike classical physics, quantum mechanics does not allow exact positions and velocities for particles; instead, particles exist as probabilities.
- The principle explains unpredictable behaviors that are fundamental to technologies like quantum computing and advanced microscopy.
