- The Byzantine Empire, often overlooked in history, was the continuation of the Roman Empire from 330 CE until its fall in 1453.
- Founded by Constantine in Constantinople, it thrived through cultural and technological advancements, but faced decline due to internal strife and external invasions, culminating in its conquest by the Ottomans.

- Most history books will tell you that the Roman Empire fell in the fifth century CE.
- However, this would have come as a great surprise to the millions of people who lived in the Roman Empire through the Middle Ages.
- This medieval Roman Empire, which we refer to today as the Byzantine Empire, began in 330 CE when Constantine, the first Christian emperor, moved the capital of the Roman Empire to a new city called Constantinople, founded on the site of the ancient Greek city Byzantion.

- The Foundation of Constantinople

- When the Visigoths sacked Rome in 410 CE and the Empire’s western provinces were conquered by various barbarian tribes, Constantine’s Eastern capital remained the seat of the Roman emperors.
- Generations of emperors ruled from Constantinople for the next 11 centuries, maintaining a continuity with the classical Roman Empire that provided the Byzantine Empire with a technological and artistic advantage over its neighbors, whom they considered barbarians.

- Cultural and Technological Advancements

- In the ninth century, visitors from beyond the Byzantine frontier were astonished by the architectural marvels of Constantinople.
- The imperial palace featured graceful stone arches and domes, with a pair of golden lions flanking the imperial throne.
- A hidden organ would make the lions roar as guests fell to their knees, while golden birds sang from a nearby golden tree.
- Byzantine engineers even utilized hydraulic engines to elevate the imperial throne high into the air.

- The influence of ancient Roman culture was evident in various aspects of Byzantine life.
- Emperors wore traditional military garb and togas, and the courts continued to uphold Roman law.
- Working-class Byzantines lived lives similar to their Ancient Roman counterparts, engaging in farming or specific trades such as ceramics, leatherworking, fishing, weaving, or silk manufacturing.

- The Byzantine Empire was not merely a continuation of Roman traditions; it was also a period of significant artistic innovation.
- Byzantine artists created vast mosaics and ornate marble carvings, and their architects constructed numerous churches.
- One of the most famous, Hagia Sophia, featured a dome so high that it was said to be hanging on a chain from heaven.

- Intellectual Contributions

- The Byzantine Empire was home to great intellectuals, such as Anna Komnene, an imperial princess in the 12th century.
- She dedicated her life to philosophy and history, and her account of her father’s reign remains historians’ foremost source for Byzantine political history during the time of the First Crusade.
- Another notable scholar, Leo the Mathematician, invented a system of beacons that spanned the width of the empire—now modern-day Greece and Turkey.
- This system, stretching over 700 kilometers, allowed the edge of the Empire to alert the emperor of invading armies within one hour of sighting them at the border.

- The Decline of the Empire

- Despite these advancements, the Byzantine Empire could not protect itself indefinitely.
- In 1203, an army of French and Venetian Crusaders made a deal with Alexios Angelos, the son of a deposed emperor, promising him vast riches and support to reclaim the throne from his uncle.
- Although Alexios succeeded, he was deposed and killed after just a year due to a rebellion from the population.
- The unpaid army then turned their aggression on Constantinople, igniting massive fires that destroyed countless works of ancient and medieval art and literature, leaving about one-third of the population homeless.

- The city was reclaimed 50 years later by the Roman Emperor Michael Palaiologos, but his restored Empire never regained all the territory lost to the Crusaders.

- The Fall of Constantinople

- The final blow to the Byzantine Empire came in 1453 when Ottoman Emperor Mehmed the Conqueror captured Constantinople, marking the conclusive end of the Roman Empire.
- Despite the Ottoman conquest, many Greek-speaking inhabitants of the Eastern Mediterranean continued to identify as Romans until the early 21st century.

- Interestingly, it was not until the Renaissance that the term "Byzantine Empire" was first used.
- For Western Europeans, the Renaissance represented a reconnection with the wisdom of antiquity.
- The existence of a medieval Roman Empire suggested that there were Europeans who had never lost touch with ancient knowledge.
- To distinguish between the classical, Latin-speaking, pagan Roman Empire and the medieval, Greek-speaking, Christian Roman Empire, scholars coined the term "Byzantine."

- Thus, 100 years after its fall, the Byzantine Empire was born in the historical narrative, forever altering our understanding of this remarkable civilization that bridged the ancient and medieval worlds.
