# What is the Double-Slit Experiment?
- The double-slit experiment demonstrates that light can fundamentally display both wave and particle features, also known as wave-particle duality.
- It consists of two closely-spaced slits in front of a light source, with a beam of light passing through them and being split to project on a screen placed far from the slits.
- The split beam forms two coherent sources of light, resulting in a pattern of bright and dark fringes on the screen due to interference of light waves from these two sources.
- Interference is the phenomenon where superimposed waves may reinforce or cancel each other out, most pronounced when the wavelength of the radiation is comparable to the linear dimensions of the slits.
- The history of this experiment dates back to 1801 when British polymath Thomas Young demonstrated the wave nature of light, often called Young’s double-slit experiment.

# Double-Slit Experiment Equation
- A monochromatic light source falls on a screen containing two parallel slits, serving as coherent sources of light.
- The light waves emerging from the two slits interfere and form an interference pattern on the viewing screen, with bright fringes corresponding to maxima and dark fringes to minima.
- By measuring the separation between fringes and knowing the distance of the screen from the slits and the separation between the slits, it is possible to determine the wavelength of light.

# Key Findings
- The double-slit experiment was a groundbreaking test that decisively proves the theory that light behaves as a wave.
- Many researchers have performed the double-slit experiment on electrons and found similar results to those observed with photons.
