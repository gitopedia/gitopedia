# Introduction
- Albert Einstein and his colleagues showed in 1935 that quantum mechanics permits "spooky action at a distance," which seemed highly suspect to physicists at the time.
- John Stewart Bell proved in 1964 that quantum mechanics predicted stronger statistical correlations than any local theory could, demoting locality from a cherished principle to a testable hypothesis.

# The Concept of Entanglement
- Entanglement involves particles losing their independence and exhibiting strongly correlated measurement outcomes, even when far apart.
- Measuring the properties of entangled particles appears to instantly affect each other's outcomes, violating locality.
- Spin is a property of electrons that can be measured along any axis, yielding binary results (up or down) for each measurement.

# Local Hidden Variables
- Einstein proposed local hidden variables as an explanation for entanglement, suggesting preexisting values for particles' spins.
- These variables could explain same-axis measurements yielding opposite results without nonlocality.

# Bell’s Theorem and Its Implications
- Bell proved that local hidden variable theories could not account for all possible measurement outcomes in quantum mechanics.
- If locality held, the probability of obtaining opposite results when measuring along different axes would be at most 67% (Bell's inequality).
- Quantum mechanics predicts stronger correlations, such as 75% same results when axes are 120 degrees apart.

# Experimental Validation
- Experiments since the 1970s have confirmed quantum mechanics' predictions, closing various loopholes in recent years.
- These findings support the conclusion that locality does not hold in our world.

# Impact of Bell’s Theorem
- Bell's theorem has led to the development of quantum technologies and a deeper understanding of nonlocality in quantum mechanics.
- It challenges deeply held intuitions about physics and opens possibilities for unimaginable tasks in a classical world.
