# Momentum and Position
- Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle states that if the uncertainty in position (Δx) is small, the uncertainty in momentum (Δp) must be large, and vice versa.
- For a free particle moving along the x-direction with constant velocity u, the wave function has a uniform probability density |ψk(x,t)|² = A², indicating equal likelihood of being found anywhere along the x-axis.
- A localized particle is modeled by a wave packet, which is a linear superposition of plane-wave states. This wave packet has many wavelengths and momenta, allowing for both position and momentum uncertainties.
- The product of the uncertainty in position (Δx) and the uncertainty in momentum (Δp) cannot be less than one-half of the reduced Planck constant: ΔxΔp ≥ ℏ/2.

# Uncertainty Principle Large and Small
- For an electron with a speed precision of 1.0×10⁻³ m/s, the minimum position uncertainty is calculated as:
  - Δp = (9.1×10⁻³¹ kg)(1.0×10⁻³ m/s) = 9.1×10⁻³⁴ kg·m/s
  - Δx = ℏ/(2Δp) ≈ 5.8 cm
- For a bowling ball with mass 6.0 kg and the same speed precision:
  - Δp = (6.0 kg)(1.0×10⁻³ m/s) = 6.0×10⁻³ kg·m/s
  - Δx = ℏ/(2Δp) ≈ 8.8×10⁻³³ m

# Uncertainty and the Hydrogen Atom
- The ground-state energy of a hydrogen atom can be estimated using Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle.
- Assuming the size of the hydrogen atom is approximately 0.1 nm, the minimum uncertainty product for position and momentum leads to an estimate of the ground-state energy:
  - E₀ ≈ ℏ²/(8mL²) = (ℏc)²/(8mc²L²)
  - Substituting values: E₀ ≈ 1 eV

# Energy and Time
- The energy-time uncertainty principle states that ΔEΔt ≥ ℏ/2, where ΔE is the uncertainty in energy and Δt is the uncertainty in lifetime.
- A quantum state with a short lifetime (Δt) has a broad range of possible energies (ΔE), leading to spectral lines with widths corresponding to their lifetimes.

# Application to Spectral Lines
- For an atom in an excited state with a lifetime Δt ≈ 10⁻⁸ s and emitting photons with average frequency f = 7.1×10¹⁴ Hz:
  - Energy uncertainty: ΔE ≈ ℏ/(2Δt) = (ℏc)/(2Δt)
  - Frequency spread: Δf ≈ ΔE/h = (ℏc)/(hΔt) = (c)/(2Δt f)
  - Calculated frequency spread: Δf ≈ 8.0×10⁶ Hz
  - Fraction of spread: Δf/f ≈ 1.1×10⁻⁸, indicating the radiation is effectively monochromatic.
