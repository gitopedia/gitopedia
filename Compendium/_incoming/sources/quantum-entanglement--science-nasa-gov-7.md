# Quantum Entanglement

## Overview
Quantum entanglement is a phenomenon in quantum mechanics where particles that originate from the same source remain interconnected, regardless of their spatial separation. This means that the quantum state of one particle is directly related to the other, even when they are far apart.

## History
The concept was first highlighted by Albert Einstein and his colleagues in 1935, who referred to it as "spooky action at a distance." Einstein viewed this phenomenon as conflicting with his theory of special relativity, which states that no information or influence can travel faster than the speed of light. This discrepancy troubled Einstein deeply.

In 1964, physicist John Stewart Bell developed a method to test quantum entanglement experimentally. His work laid the foundation for later experiments that confirmed the existence of quantum entanglement.

## Key Concepts
Quantum entanglement operates in contrast to classical physics, where objects have well-defined states (like an apple staying on a table). In the quantum realm, particles can exist in multiple states simultaneously and be entangled with each other. When one particle's state is measured, the other instantly assumes the corresponding state, regardless of the distance between them.

## Applications
Quantum entanglement has implications for fields such as quantum computing, cryptography, and sensing. NASA has conducted experiments like the SEAQUE (Space Entanglement and Annealing Quantum Experiment) to explore its potential applications in space. These studies aim to advance our understanding of quantum mechanics and develop technologies that could revolutionize space exploration and life on Earth.

Quantum entanglement remains a mysterious yet fundamental aspect of quantum physics, continuing to inspire scientific research and technological innovation.
