# Tidal Energy  

## Historical Context  
- Tidal energy is power produced by the surge of ocean waters during the rise and fall of tides.  
- It is a renewable energy source, harnessed through technologies like turbines and paddles.  
- The concept of using tidal movement for electricity generation began in the 20th century, with engineers developing methods to exploit significant tidal ranges.  
- The first commercial tidal power plant was built in La Rance, France, in 1966.  
- The largest operational tidal power station is Sihwa Lake in South Korea, which began operations in 2008.  
- The United States has no commercial tidal energy plants, though legal and regulatory challenges persist due to ownership disputes and environmental concerns.  

## Technologies  
### Tidal Streams  
- Tidal stream technology captures energy from the kinetic motion of water as it flows in and out with the tides.  
- Turbines are submerged in tidal channels or near coastal areas to convert the movement of water into electricity.  
- This method is similar to wind energy but uses underwater turbines instead of above-ground windmills.  

### Barrages  
- A tidal barrage is a structure built across an estuary or bay to control the flow of water between the sea and the tidal basin.  
- Barrages use gates to store water during high tide and release it during low tide, generating electricity through turbines.  
- The Rance River tidal power plant in France is a classic example, with a 240-meter-long barrage that produces 240 MW of power.  
- Barrages can disrupt local ecosystems and alter sediment transport, requiring careful environmental planning.  

### Tidal Lagoons  
- Tidal lagoons are artificial enclosures designed to trap water during high tide and release it during low tide, generating electricity through turbines.  
- Unlike barrages, lagoons are often built as standalone structures rather than across natural estuaries.  
- The proposed Swansea Bay Tidal Lagoon in Wales (planned for 2018) aimed to demonstrate the feasibility of this technology.  
- Tidal lagoons can be more environmentally friendly than barrages, as they minimize disruption to natural tidal patterns.  

### Dynamic Tidal Power (DTP)  
- DTP is a newer concept involving long, semi-submerged barriers that channel tidal currents into enclosed areas, creating a pressure differential to drive turbines.  
- This method theoretically allows for larger energy capture than traditional tidal technologies but remains in the experimental or planning stages.  

## Environmental Impact  
- Tidal energy is considered a clean, renewable energy source with minimal greenhouse gas emissions.  
- However, it can disrupt marine ecosystems, alter sediment transport, and affect fish migration patterns.  
- The Rance River tidal power plant has been studied for its impact on local wildlife, including changes in fish populations and sedimentation.  
- Tidal barrages may block the movement of marine species, requiring mitigation strategies such as fish-friendly turbines or bypass channels.  
- Tidal lagoons and DTP systems are designed to minimize ecological disruption by avoiding large-scale habitat alteration.  

## Economic and Legal Challenges  
- Tidal energy projects face high initial costs due to the need for robust infrastructure and specialized equipment.  
- The U.S. lacks a comprehensive regulatory framework for tidal energy, leading to legal uncertainties about water rights and environmental permits.  
- Investors are hesitant due to the long payback periods and technical challenges of scaling tidal energy projects.  
- International examples like the Sihwa Lake plant demonstrate the potential for tidal energy to contribute to national energy grids, but such projects require significant government and private sector collaboration.  

## Related Concepts and Definitions  
- **Tidal Bore**: A sudden, strong movement of water that occurs when the incoming tide forms a wave that travels up a river or narrow bay.  
- **Tidal Range**: The difference in height between an area's high tide and low tide.  
- **Tidal Current**: An ocean current produced by the tide, which can be harnessed for energy.  
- **Turbine**: A machine that converts the kinetic energy of moving water or air into electricity.  
- **Tidal Energy**: Energy obtained from the movement of ocean waters caused by the gravitational pull of the moon and sun.  
- **Tidal Lagoon**: A man-made structure that traps water during high tide and releases it during low tide to generate electricity.  
- **Tidal Power Plant**: An industrial facility that generates electricity using the energy of tides.  
- **Tidal Stream**: A steady, predictable flow of water within a larger body of water, often used to generate energy.  
- **Tidal Barrage**: A structure built across an estuary or bay to control the flow of water and generate electricity.  
- **Tidal Energy Technologies**: A set of methods, including tidal streams, barrages, and lagoons, used to harness energy from ocean tides.  
- **Tidal Range**: The vertical difference between high tide and low tide in a given location.  
- **Tidal Currents**: The movement of water caused by the gravitational pull of the moon and sun, which can be harnessed for energy.  
- **Tidal Energy**: Energy derived from the rise and fall of ocean tides, considered a renewable resource.  
- **Tidal Power**: The process of generating electricity from the energy of tides, often using turbines or barrages.  
- **Tidal Energy Systems**: Technologies and infrastructure designed to capture and convert tidal energy into usable electricity.
