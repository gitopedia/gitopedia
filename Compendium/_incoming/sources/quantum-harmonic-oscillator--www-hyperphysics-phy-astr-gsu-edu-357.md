# Quantum Harmonic Oscillator
- A diatomic molecule vibrates somewhat like two masses on a spring with a potential energy that depends upon the square of the displacement from equilibrium.  
- The energy levels of the quantum harmonic oscillator are quantized at equally spaced values.  
- For a diatomic molecule, the natural frequency is of the form \( \omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{\mu}} \), where \( k \) is the force constant and \( \mu \) is the reduced mass.  
- The reduced mass is given by \( \mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2} \).  
- This form of the frequency is the same as that for the classical simple harmonic oscillator.  
- The most surprising difference for the quantum case is the so-called "zero-point vibration" of the \( n=0 \) ground state.  
- This implies that molecules are not completely at rest, even at absolute zero temperature.  
- The quantum harmonic oscillator has implications far beyond the simple diatomic molecule.  
- It is the foundation for the understanding of complex modes of vibration in larger molecules.  
- It is also fundamental to the motion of atoms in a solid lattice and the theory of heat capacity.  
- In real systems, energy spacings are equal only for the lowest levels where the potential is a good approximation of the "mass on a spring" type harmonic potential.  
- The anharmonic terms which appear in the potential for a diatomic molecule are useful for mapping the detailed potential of such systems.
