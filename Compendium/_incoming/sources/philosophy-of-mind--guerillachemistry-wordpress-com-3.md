# Philosophy of Mind

- A Brief History of the Mind: From Early Modern Philosophy to the dawn of Behaviorism

- „You′re searching for your mind don’t know where to start“

- Black Sabbath, Lord of This World

- „The subject does not belong to the world, but it is a limit of the world“

- Ludwig Wittgenstein, Tractatus logico-philosophicus

- This paper explores a historical perspective on the concept of the mind, tracing its evolution from Descartes’ dualism to the rise of behaviorism.
- By examining key philosophical figures—Descartes, Locke, Berkeley, Hume, and Kant—the paper highlights a gradual shift towards positivism and increasing skepticism about the metaphysical nature of the mind.
- It argues that behaviorism’s dismissal of introspection and focus on observable behavior were shaped by centuries of philosophical debates.
- Despite these advances, the question of consciousness remains unresolved.
- Empiricist philosophy however is offering a foundation for ongoing inquiry in cognitive sciences.

- The nature of the mind is one of the central questions in philosophy.
- Since the early modern period, thinking has been explored in radically new ways, and the discussion of the concept of the mind has sparked numerous debates that continue to this day.
- From Descartes’ rationalism to British empiricism, Kant’s critical philosophy, and later behaviorism, ideas about consciousness and its relationship to the world have continuously evolved.
- This paper examines this development and traces the key positions that paved the way for the modern sciences of the mind—or, more precisely, the essential part of our mind that we call the “self.” The key takeaway of my argument is that the behaviorist critique of the mental and the mind was preceded by hundreds of years of philosophical development.
- As will be shown, a general trend has been increasing positivism, leading to a more negative1 conception of the mind.
- Thus, in a sense, behaviorism is indebted to the philosophical tradition.

- This paper explores conceptions of the mind, but to do so meaningfully, they must be understood within their broader philosophical context.
- The thinkers’ “minds” cannot be examined in isolation, as they are embedded in philosophical frameworks.
- As will become particularly evident in Hume’s work, any discussion of the mind is inherently tied to broader epistemological and ontological questions.

- 2.0 René Descartes’ res cogitans at the beginning of modern philosophy

- René Descartes [1596-1650] is often credited with kickstarting modern western philosophy. Browse thinkers after Descartes, no matter if Locke, Kant or Nietzsche, and you notice how ubiquitous his influence as well as his haters are.2

- The rationalist3 René Descartes’ famous Meditationes de prima philosophia claims that only reason, via innate principles, can produce secure knowledge—that is, knowledge of the nature of things (see Hatfield 2023: 3.1).
- Truth, according to Descartes, can be perceived both distinctly and clearly through the action of the intellect (which holds content) and the will, which affirms it.
- When we encounter certain truths, the will affirms them compulsively (see Hatfield 2023: 3.2).
- These innate truths or ideas are always of a metaphysical kind (see Hatfield 2023: 3.1), for example, the idea of causation (see Markie 2021: 4.0).
- The superiority of innate ideas is of great importance to Descartes’ universal doubt, which disqualifies the senses as a source of infallible knowledge.

- I presume some familiarity with Descartes’ cogito argument at this point.
- So I won’t go into detail, but extract three much-argued-over points.
- Descartes’ analysis of him experiencing himself as a subject concludes that thinking is the essential property of his mind: „[…]from the consciousness that nothing besides thinking belongs to the essence of the mind, it follows that nothing else does in truth belong to it.“ (Descartes 1901: 8 f.).
- Furthermore Descartes claims that mind and body are two different substances that have two different essences and thus can exist independently (See Descartes 1901: 12 f.).

- 2.1 The cartesian subject

- Descartes is quick to define his subject as „ […] a real thing, and really existent; but what thing? The answer was, a thinking thing“ (Descartes 1901: 24). Note how Descartes himself notices how his supposedly foundational assertion about his mind brings about counterintuitive consequences:

- „But, nevertheless, it still seems to me, and I cannot help believing, that corporeal things, whose images are formed by thought which fall under the senses, and are examined by the same, are known with much greater distinctness than that I know not what part of myself which is not imaginable; although, in truth, it may seem strange to say that I know and comprehend with greater distinctness things whose existence appears to me doubtful, that are unknown, and do not belong to me, than others of whose reality I am persuaded, that are known to me, and appertain to my proper nature; in a word, than myself“ (Descartes 1901: 26).

- Descartes following argument for sure is consistent concerning some4 form ofexistence of the self as a thinking think:

- I who seem to possess so distinct an apprehension of the piece of wax, do I not know myself, both with greater truth and certitude, and also much more distinctly and clearly?
- For if I judge that the wax exists because I see it, it assuredly follows, much more evidently, that I myself am or exist, for the same reason: for it is possible that what I see may not in truth be wax, and that I do not even possess eyes with which to see anything; but it cannot be that when I see, or, which comes to the same thing, when I think I see, I myself who think am nothing“ (Descartes 1901: 30).

- Still, one could claim that this is totally begging the question.
- All we get is a really abstract notion of the mind.
- How can we know that the analysis of the mind as a thinking think is exhaustive if we don’t observe it distinctly and clearly as an entity?
- Don’t the clarity and distinctness of statements about the mind depend on a clear and distinct vision or representation of the mind in the mind?
- In my opinion, Descartes doesn’t do justice to his own demands here.
- Positive assertions about the mind, as we will see before long, weren’t the vogue after Descartes (at least in Britain), and especially Berkeley and Hume produced good arguments against them.5

- Although Descartes philosophy didn’t meet general agreement of the philosophical community in the long-term, it effectively helped furnish new debates and branches of philosophy independent of the dominant scholastic schools of the Middle Ages (See Hatfield 2023: 3.1).
- How the discussion of the mind becomes central to modern philosophical discourse after Descartes I will try to show in this paper, while paying attention to the differences in the conception of the subject, soul, spirit, self or mind that distinguish later philosophers from Descartes.
- Be aware that I will treat these terms as largely synonymous, because the philosophers who I want to discuss don’t really differentiate between these.
- Best read them as undifferentiated for the purpose of this paper, and forget the definitions you have in mind.

- Before we turn from the archetype of the rationalist to the rivaling empiricist philosophers, I want to add a quote that helps understanding the relation of both camps:

- „Locke, like Descartes, is tearing down the foundations of the old Aristotelian scholastic house of knowledge. But while Descartes focused on the empiricism at the foundation of the structure, Locke is focusing on the claims that innate ideas provide its first principles.“ (Uzgalis 2022: 2.1)

- 3.0 Locke, Berkeley, Hume and their new method

- 3.1 A short history of the three British empiricists: A drama in three acts

- Often, John Locke [1632-1704], George Berkeley [1685-1753] and David Hume [1711-1776] are framed as thetriumvirate of British empiricism, ignoring the likes of Francis Bacon and Thomas Hobbes.
- An objective all three of them share is delineating the borders of what can be known from what can’t.
- As they are sensualists, they claim that the senses give us access to ideas and thus knowledge.

- The British empiricists are united by two theses, one negative and one positive:

- There is no innate knowledge

- Knowledge is gained by experience6, not inscribed in the soul and later on triggered (See Markie 2021: 1.2)

- Elsewhere, they disagree on a number of issues.
- These concern the existence of perception-independent matter (in Kant’s words: Dinge an sich) and properties, which Locke postulates (see Locke 1974: 24 f.) but which Berkeley (see Berkeley 1974: 154) and, to some degree, Hume (see Hume 1974: 422) reject.
- One of Locke’s central ontological points is his corpuscular theory of material reality.
- He argues that invisible small particles, which exist independently of being perceived, somehow trigger perception through interaction with our senses.
- In accordance with Descartes (see Hatfield 2023: 5.0), he claims that so-called primary qualities of matter, like extension and motion, exist even when nobody perceives the object.
- In comparison, color or smell, according to Locke—so-called secondary qualities—depend on being perceived (see Locke 1974: 24 f.).

- His intellectual successor, Berkeley, rejects the distinction between primary and secondary qualities (see Berkeley 1974: 154).
- In stark contrast to Locke, Berkeley argues that “esse is percipi” (Berkeley 1974: 152)—being is being perceived—thereby denying any reality beyond the testimony of our senses.
- For Berkeley, all that truly exists are ideas and minds: the perceived and the perceiver (see Berkeley 1974: 153).
- Thus, perception-independent objects like atoms and Dinge an sich (as well as their properties) cannot exist because they are not perceived.
- This applies equally to Locke’s primary qualities as well as the secondary qualities.

- What initially might not sound like an empiricist theory is, upon closer inspection, arguably more radically and consistently committed to empiricist theses than Locke’s.
- The trouble with Locke, as Berkeley realized, is precisely that he postulates things that cannot be perceived, thereby demonstrating a lack of radical commitment to empiricist or sensualist theses.

- Lockes branch of empiricism relies heavily on non-perceivableabstract ideas, whose existence he deduces from perceptions (See Markie 2021: 0.0). Berkeley defends the position that abstract ideas and thus the lockean matter doesn’t exist (See Berkeley 1974: 154).

- Berkeley’s philosophy is summed up best by himself: „That what I see, hear, and feel doth exist, that is to say, is perceived by me, I no more doubt than I do of my own being.
- But I do not see how the testimony of sense can be alleged as a proof for the existence of anything which is not perceived by sense.“ (Berkeley 1974: 166).
- Here Berkeley is opposed to both Locke’s empiricism and Descartes’ rationalism (at least concerning the foundation of the latters doubt).

- According to Berkeley, then, what stabilizes the world that supposedly is of ideal nature?
- Why isn’t the world of dream-like character?7 What guarantees causality and ordering laws of nature?
- For Berkeley, a theologist, the answer seems natural: God (See Berkeley 1974: 162).
- If you read Berkeley’s A treatise concerning the principles of human knowledge you will be astonished by how this argument pops out of thin air mid-book.

- Hume, not committed to religiosity in the same way as Berkeley, can’t accept his explanation concerning causality and uniformity of nature.
- In An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, he famously askshow we arrive at inductive inferences, which are the basis not only of empirical science but of our daily lives as well.8 „What is the foundation of all conclusions from experience?“ (Hume 1974: 328), is the central question of Chapter IV and V of his Enquiry.
- In empiricist fashion, he investigates the original impression from which we derive the idea of causation.
- He does so because a term that doesn’t refer to experience, since Berkeley, is considered hollow.
- He can’t find the original impression he searched for (See Hume 1974: 322-346).
- Thus, the term „causality“ is rendered meaningless: „[…] and we only learn by experience the frequent conjunction of objects, without being ever able to comprehend anything like connection between them.“ (Hume 1974: 356 f.).
- So We never encounter an original impression of a link between cause and effect.

- Hume later on argues that inductive inferences are but a habit, something we do because of a natural instinct (See Hume 1974: 338 f.).
- As Peter Markie puts it: „Our claims, […], about causal connections in the world turn out, given the limited content of our empirically based concept of causation, to be claims and knowledge about the constant conjunction of events and our own feeling of expectation.“ (Markie 2021: 4.0) Furthermore, he shows that justifying the legitimacy of induction is neither a priori nor a posteriori consistentlypossible (See Hume 1974: 332).

- What Hume does here, is essentially carrying the project of empiricism to it’s grave.
- He proofs that the very precondition of the empirical endeavor, the uniformity of nature as well as causality, can’t be proven to exist by empirical methods.
- What is even worse – according to Hume, the rationalists can’t proof it either!
- Up to this day, philosophers are arguing on how to solve Hume’s problem of induction.
- Hans Reichenbach, who essentially agrees with Humes dilemma but tries to diffuse it comments: „Hume’s criticism was the heaviest blow against empiricism;[…]“ (Reichenbach 2006 : 347)

- Note how the empiricists tried to improve their predecessors coherence by applying sensualist methods in a more and more radical fashion.
- In the end, what Hume, at least according to Kant’s interpretation encountered, was the very incoherence of empiricism itself.
- A similar evolution of the empiricists coherence, albeit with equally radical implications, can be illustrated regarding the mind.

- 3.2 John Locke vs. René Descartes

- The first of the three famous British Empiricists, John Locke, can’t be categorized as a substance monist or substance dualist easily.

- First and foremost, consciousness is central to Lockes definition of a person: „…,we must consider what person stands for;-which, I think, is a thinking intelligent being, that has reason and reflection, and can consider itself as itself; […] which it does only by that consciousness which is inseparable from thinking, and, as it seems to me, essential to it: […]“ (Locke 1974: 67).
- Thus consciousness, Locke explains, is constitutive of a person.9 I think we are allowed to deduce here, given we can define the self as that which considers „itself as itself“, that consciousness is central to selfhood too.
- This is of course somewhat broader than Locke’s definition of the person, but Locke’s further qualifications that define the stronger „person“ can be neglected to get his point.
- In at least four central aspects Locke’s philosophy of mind departs from Descartes claims about the immaterial res cogitans:

- By speculating that God could as well have created thinking matter10 (See Locke 1974: 84 f.)

- By claiming that thinking is not an essential part of consciousness11, but the other way around (See Locke 1974: 13 and 67 f.)

- By claiming that the mind doesn’t exist uninterrupted but is fragmented (See Locke 1974: 68), thus challenging numerical identity of the self

- By claiming that thinking and perception are the same (See Locke 1974: 21)

- The first claim is quite interesting because it almost reads like a hint towards multiple realizability; although we have to note here that what Locke has in mind is the two options of immaterial and material realization, which, for him, seem equally possible.
- Locke’s second claim essentially robs Descartes’ res cogitans of what Descartes held to be it’s defining characteristic that guarantees its being over time: Thinking.
- Locke simply argues – in a manner the other two British empiricists do to – by reference to experience.
- Locke by reflection concludes that his soul is not always thinking, as thinking is just one of its operations (See Locke 1974: 13); he grounds that claim in arguing that thinking is necessarily conscious.12 So if we were thinking all the time, we would have reflective awareness of our thoughts all the time.
- Descartes on the other hand beliefs that there are thoughts we don’t have reflective awareness of (See Hatfield 2023: 3.3).

- In the third claim Locke notes that during good sleep, consciousness is interrupted and so is thinking, and it’s unclear wether we are the same thinking substance wenn we wake up (See Locke 1974: 68).
- What Locke exactly means by thinking becomes clear from the fourth claim, he however claims that thinking is nevertheless mysterious (See Locke 1974: 20).
- The fourth claim exemplifies the radical nature of the empiricist paradigm well.

- Even more pressing, right at the beginning of his essay, he even claims that like an eye cannot see itself, the mind has trouble perceiving itself ((See Locke 1974: 7). Here Locke is taking up something Descartes pointed out but did not elaborate earlier.

- In general, Locke seems somewhat agnostic concerning whether the mind is material or immaterial in nature.13 Still, some of his accounts seem to contradict his speculative thesis that matter could possibly think.
- So a pinch of dualism is still present in his theory.
- Locke is solving the problem, again, by reference to the omnipotence of God (See Uzgalis 2022 (2): 0.0).

- 3.3 George Berkeley: The mind as an active, ineffable entity

- Leaving aside Berkeleys critique of Locke’s ontology, he has an interesting perspective of the mind to offer.
- As we already discussed, Berkeley agrees with the other two empiricists that all knowledge is gained by the senses.
- But – all we perceive, according to Berkeley, are ideas.
- And these ideas exist because we perceive them.
- Now, according to Berkeley, ideas are passive in character.
- Conversely, the mind (here spirit) which perceives, is active:

- „A spirit is one simple, undivided, active being: as it perceives ideas it is called the understanding, and as it produces or otherwise operates about them it is called the will.“ (Berkeley 1974: 161)

- Thus, Berkeley explains, we can’t form an idea of the mind – because ideas are passive, and the mind is active.14 The negative side of Berkeleys statement emphasizes exactly that:

- „Such is the nature of spirit, or that which acts, that it cannot be of itself perceived, but only by the effects which it produceth.“ (Berkeley 1974: 161)

- What Berkeley underscores here is that the mind is not a thing among things (not an idea among ideas), justifying the very impossibility of self-perception Descartes stumbled upon. In other words: The mind can never be the object of the senses, and for that reason, it can never be inquired.

- We can reframe our suspicion towards Descartes supposedly seeing his thinking thing so clearly now: The thinking think ist not a thing. What Descartes might be guilty of is reification of an entity that isn’t a res.

- We’ve got to halt here and lock back.
- Berkeley noticed that Locke’s assumption of mind-independent matter is not consistent in an empiricist framework because he postulates something that is not observable by the senses.
- But is Berkeley not doing the same?
- As becomes evident from my interpretation, Berkeleys subject is not an object of the senses – so he can’t claim by reference to experience that it exists.

- We already considered how David Hume challenges the nature of Berkeley’s ontology by stripping it of an almighty God and consequently found it to be quite fragile. Of course, he has to weigh in with his opinion concerning this matter too.

- 3.3 David Hume and the nonexistence of the self

- Hopefully I could demonstrate two trends in British empiricism so far.
- The first one, already discussed, is the strive towards consistence or coherence.
- The second one is increasing positivism, skepticism concerning postulated entities that cannot be observed.
- David Hume was the philosopher carrying that attitude to extremes.
- In the same manner he investigated non-observable causality, he proceeds to investigate what he calls the self:

- „Some philosophers believe this: We are every moment intimately conscious of what we call our self ; we feel its existence and its continuing to exist, and are certain—more even than any demonstration could make us—both of its perfect identity and of its simplicity.“ (Hume 2017: 130)

- This passage could be read as aiming both at Berkeleys conception of the mind we just discussed, as well as Descartes’. Hume’s method unfolds it’s full force with the gloves off:

- „Unfortunately, all these forthright assertions are in conflict with the very experience that is supposed to support them. We don’t so much as have an idea of self of the kind that is here described. From what impression could this idea be derived?“ (Hume 2017: 130)

- Of course, there is no original impression of the mind, according to Hume. Thus, the term is meaningless (in the framework of radical empiricism). Instead, what is truly observable, are ever-changing perceptions:

- „But setting aside metaphysicians of this kind, I am willing to affirm of the rest of mankind that each of us is nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions that follow each other enormously quickly and are in a perpetual flux and movement.
- Our eyes can’t turn in their sockets without varying our perceptions; our thought is even more variable than our sight; and all our other senses and faculties contribute to this change in our perceptions, with no one of them remaining unaltered for a moment.
- The mind is a kind of stage on which many perceptions successively make their appearance: they pass back and forth, glide away, and mingle in an infinite variety of positions and situations.“ (Hume 2017: 131 f.)

- What Hume reveals here, is that there is no „master-thought“ (Priest 2016: 170) to find by empiricist introspection. That plain observation, as has been shown, has a history and is a consequence of the increasing consistency of empiricism.

- This is an odd result, at least for a western philosopher.
- Often it has been pointed out that Hume is close to Mahayana Buddhist conceptions of the mind here (See Priest 2016: 168-181, Siderits 2021: 55 f.).
- Of course that would be interesting to investigate, but I chose to focus on the western tradition in this paper.15

- David Hume is one of the ancestors of modern positivism, including positivists in the 19th century and the logical positivist movement since Wittgenstein.
- As we shall see in 4.3., his lasting influence concerning the mind is not only present in schools of analytic philosophy, but in its cousin, the behaviorist movement, too.
- But first, I want to explore Immanuel Kant’s conception of the mind shortly, as it can be read as a direct answer to Humes positivist challenge.
- Although Kant somehow disagrees with Hume concerning the existence of a mind, his conception of the mind is even more challenging to a possible science of the mind.
- As Hume attacks the subjective side of the mind, Kant denies its objective existence as an object of science in the empirical world.

- 3.5 The Aftermath of British empiricism: Rationalism strikes back

- Immanuel Kant’s [1724-1804] critical philosophy in Kritik der reinen Vernunft was largely triggered after he, himself a rationalist before, read Humes Enquiry.
- Kant agrees with Hume that pure sensation or experience is unable to provide us an idea or notion of the mind or causality.
- Kants solution is to show how both notions are innate.
- For Kant, the mind is the necessary precondition for the empirical world because it unifies experience.
- The mind imposes innate categories like causality and substance on the world, thus structuring raw sensory data into coherent objects.
- Without this self-conscious “I” synthesizing perceptions, there would be no intelligible world—only an unconnected flow of impressions [See Scruton 2001: 33-53].
- In other words: The mental architecture of the mind is the frame of the world.

- In the light of that insight Kant’s famous quote: „Gedanken ohne Inhalt sind leer, Anschauungen ohne Begriffe sind blind.“ (Kant 1781: 51) has to be understood.
- According to Kant, reason is dependent on sensation to produce knowledge.
- Answering Hume, he claims that reality is dependent on innate notions structuring it.
- Thus Hume, according to Kant, was right in concluding that pure empiricism has problematic consequences.
- Kants answer was a mild form of innateness, while making sensation a precondition for Erkenntnis as well.16

- Now, according to Kant, as the mind is a precondition of experience, it is, ontologically speaking, outside of the world. It provides a formal structure that enables experience but is not itself possibly an object of experience within this world.

- Thus, Kant holds that locating the mind in the physical brain is impossible (see Breidbach 1997: 45 f.).

- 4.0 From philosophy to the hard sciences

- 4.1 George Romanes pro Kant and Hume

- Both Humes and Kants views on the mind gained momentum quickly. George Romanes [1848-1894], a friend of Charles Darwin, in 1884 defends a synthesis of both:

- „It is obvious, then, to start with, that by Mind we may mean two very different things, according as we contemplate it in our own individual selves, or as manifested by other beings.
- For if I contemplate my own mind, I have an immediate cognizance of a certain flow of thoughts and feelings, which are the most ultimate things—and, indeed, the only things—of which I am cognizant.
- But if I contemplate Mind in other persons or organisms, I can have no such immediate cognizance of their thoughts and feelings; I can only infer the existence of such thoughts and feelings from the activities of the persons or organisms which appear to manifest them“ (Romanes 1884: 15).

- Evident from this part is that he shares the Humean critique of the singular mind.
- Berkeleys negative thesis on the mind is echoing here as well.
- Remember, that according to Berkeley the Mind can’t be „of itself perceived, but only by the effects which it produceth.“ (Berkeley 1974: 161).
- But he agrees with Kant as well in that the mind can never become object of the outer senses:

- „[…] it will be apparent that our knowledge of mental activities in any organism other than our own is neither subjective nor objective. [….] That it is not objective may be rendered obvious by a few moments’ reflection.
- For it is evident that mental activities in other organisms can never be to us objects of direct knowledge; as I have just said, we can only infer their existence from the objective sources supplied by observable activities of such organisms.
- Therefore all our knowledge of mental activities other than our own really consists of an inferential interpretation of bodily activities—this interpretation being founded on our subjective knowledge of our own mental activities“ (Romanes 1884: 16).

- What we can tell from that example is that negative philosophical conceptions on the mind found their way into the natural sciences, out of the ivory tower of contemplation, and influenced their methodology as well.

- 4.2 Kant and methodological Behaviorism – can the mind be a scientific object?

- The inference of mental states of other persons by reference to ones own would soon become discredited.
- In times of aspiring positivism, behaviorism radicalized both the humean and a part of the kantian thesis once more.
- John Watson’s [1878–1958] methodological behaviorism dictates a new paradigm: „Mental states are private entities which, given the necessary publicity of science, do not form proper objects of empirical study“ (Graham 2023: 2.0).
- Here Kant would surely agree, as his transcendental17 subject can never become an empirical object.18

- Methodological behaviorism has a normative attitude towards psychology: It should be the science of behavior, not of mental states (See Graham 2023: 2.0). As our body is part of the empirical world, Kant would surely agree that there can be a science of physical behavior.

- In general, calling Kant a behaviorist would be a hot take.
- Of course he isn’t, but both methodological behaviorism and Kant share negative assumptions concerning the mind dwelling as a scientific object in the external world.
- But Kants claim implies an even stronger thesis: Even neural correlates of the mind are not the mind, while behaviorism in general doesn’t seem opposed to the project of finding consciousness in the brain.
- Kant is definitely a dualist in comparison to the likes of B.F.
- Skinner, at least according to our rather superficial interpretation of the philosopher.

- 4.3 Hume and psychological Behaviorism – is there even a mind?

- Comparing behaviorism to Hume might make more intuitive sense, as both Hume and psychological behaviorism emphasize the import of learning (or conditioning) for behavior.
- Both are committed to hardcore empiricist doctrines.
- Furthermore, Hume (and Locke) defended classical associationism, claiming that „intelligent behavior is the product of associative learning.
- As a result of associations or pairings between perceptual experiences or stimulations on the one hand, and ideas or thoughts on the other, persons and animals acquire knowledge of their environment and how to act.“ (Graham 2023: 3.0).
- Classical associationism is seen as a precursor to modern behaviorism (See Graham 2023: 3.0).

- But there is another aspect, where behaviorism can be traced to one of Humes – or even Berkeleys – results.
- As was shown, Kant claims the mind doesn’t have an objective side (it cannot be materialized) while Hume claims, there is no subjective master-thought or I.
- Of course, mosts behaviorists stop short of launching a full-blown attack on the supposed unifying, subjective side of the mind.
- Skinner is more daring claiming talk about a self implies animism: „Traces of the doctrine survive when we speak of a personality, of an ego in ego psychology, of an I who says he knows what he is going to do and uses his body to do it,[…]“ [Skinner 1974: 167].
- Skinner instead favors the word person, defined as „a member of the human species, which has acquired a repertoire of behavior“ [Skinner 1974: 167].
- Thus the person is a „locus“ [Skinner 1974: 168] where genetic and environmental conditions meet [See Skinner 1974: 168].

- A bias against such a thing as the mind as a thing, ontologically speaking, is present, as the mind cannot be accounted for in terms of behavior.
- As assertions about mental states are considered inexact19 and/ or meaningless20, equally must be talk about the existence of a mind.
- Thus, existence of a mind cannot be the premise of any scientific argument, as Alan Turing, here on a par with behaviorism, pointed out as well [See Turing 1950, 445 f.].

- What I’ve got to point out here as well is that psychological behaviorisms epistemological claim is an even stronger one than Humes.
- Hume allows talk about mental states and frequently employs it.21 In general, introspection is an integral part of the British empiricist toolkit of methods.
- It is evident that the main difference between Hume’s ontology of the mind and the behaviorists is that Hume considers introspection a valid form of empirical investigation.
- Here it seems natural to question whether the behaviorist ban of talking about mental states this is even justified within the empiricist paradigm.
- I leave this question to the reader.

- My goal by now was showing that behaviorism didn’t come out of thin air when Ivan Pavlov accidentally conditioned a dog.
- Instead, I tried to embed it’s assumptions in a story that – in the European tradition – started with Descartes.
- The ontology of behaviorism developed gradually over four hundred years and is linked to the history of philosophy from the early modern period up to today.
- Increasing positivism at least in the empirical tradition went hand in hand with a more negative conception of the mind.

- If I had more space, I would link behaviorism to – as what was claimed before – is its philosophical cousin, logical positivism. Concerning the question I left open to the reader, Wittgensteins influential semantics found in the Tractatus could shed some light on the issue.

- The evolution of the philosophy of mind from Descartes to behaviorism reveals an increasing tendency to reduce the concept of the mind.
- While Descartes conceived of an immaterial res cogitans, the British empiricists attempted to explain the nature of thinking through introspection and experience.
- Hume pushed this approach to its limits by questioning the existence of a coherent self.
- Kant, in turn, sought to synthesize rationalism and empiricism by anchoring the self as a condition of experience.
- These debates paved the way for scientific psychology and behaviorism, which largely excluded the mind from inquiry and focused on observable behavior.
- Thus, the development that began with questioning the nature of the mind, turning more and more positivistic over a period of about 400 years, culminated in behaviorist psychology.

- However, the riddle of the mind remains unresolved.
- Despite undoubtable philosophical and scientific advances, the nature of consciousness is still a mystery.
- The philosophical debates of the early modern period framed our current understanding, as I argued, but the discussion is far from over.
- I bet we can learn a lot from the empiricist perspective, however.
