# What is Quantum Entanglement?
- Quantum entanglement refers to the phenomenon where aspects of one particle in an entangled pair depend on aspects of the other particle, regardless of the distance between them. 
- The particles could be electrons or photons, and their shared aspect could be spin direction.
- When a measurement is made on one particle, it instantly determines a corresponding property of the other particle, even if they are millions of light years apart.

# Historical Context
- The concept was first introduced by Albert Einstein, Boris Podolsky, and Nathan Rosen in 1935 through their thought experiment known as the EPR paradox.
- This paradox challenged foundational laws of physics, suggesting faster-than-light communication, which Einstein dismissed as "spooky action at a distance."

# Quantum Superposition
- Particles exist in multiple states simultaneously until measured. For example, spin can be both up and down until measured.
- The measurement collapses the superposition into one state, with probabilities determining the likelihood of each outcome.

# Entangled Particle Creation
- Entanglement occurs when a system is split into two parts, such that the combined system's properties are known (e.g., total spin zero).
- Each part must have opposite properties to maintain this balance. For example, if one particle has spin up, the other must be spin down.

# Thought Experiments
- The decay of a pi meson produces an electron and positron with opposite spins moving apart.
- If the electron's spin is measured as up, the positron's spin collapses to down, and vice versa.

# Hidden Variables Theory
- Einstein and others proposed hidden variables as unknown properties that predetermined particle states before measurement.
- Bell’s inequality was developed by John Bell in the 1960s to test if local hidden variable theories could explain quantum mechanics.

# Experimental Validation
- The first experiments testing Bell’s inequality were conducted by Alain Aspect, John Clauser, and Anton Zeilinger using entangled photons.
- These experiments ruled out local hidden variables, confirming quantum mechanics' predictions about entanglement.

# Implications for Physics
- Quantum entanglement does not allow faster-than-light communication, preserving special relativity.
- The correlation of measurements at a distance does not transmit information but demonstrates non-local correlations.

# Practical Applications
- Physicists continue to explore applications in fields like quantum computing and cryptography.
- Despite its mysteries, quantum mechanics remains the most accurate framework for describing entanglement.

# Recognition
- The 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded to Alain Aspect, John Clauser, and Anton Zeilinger for their work on quantum entanglement.
