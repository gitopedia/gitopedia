# What is quantum entanglement?
- Quantum entanglement is a phenomenon where two or more particles become correlated so that measuring one instantly affects the other, regardless of distance.
- Entangled particles share a quantum state and behave as a single system, even when separated.
- Einstein referred to this phenomenon as "spooky action at a distance."
- Understanding entanglement helps comprehend how quantum systems behave and why it challenges classical intuitions about space and time.

# Scientific Principles
- **Correlated states**: Entangled particles are created together and share a quantum state. Their properties are correlated—knowing one particle's state instantly tells you the other's state.
- **Instant correlation**: When you measure one entangled particle, the other's state is instantly determined, regardless of distance. This happens faster than light could travel between them.
- **No hidden information**: Entanglement doesn't involve hidden information or faster-than-light communication. The correlation is established when particles are created, not when measured.
- **Bell's theorem**: Experiments confirm that entanglement involves genuine quantum correlations, not classical hidden variables, demonstrating quantum mechanics' non-local nature.
- **Fragility**: Entanglement is fragile—interaction with the environment (decoherence) destroys entanglement, making it challenging to maintain in practical applications.

# Practical Applications
- **Quantum computing**: Quantum computers use entanglement—entangled qubits enable parallel processing and quantum algorithms impossible for classical computers.
- **Quantum cryptography**: Secure communication uses entanglement—quantum key distribution relies on entanglement to detect eavesdropping and ensure security.
- **Quantum sensors**: Entanglement can improve sensor precision—entangled particles can measure quantities more accurately than classical sensors.
- **Fundamental research**: Understanding entanglement helps study quantum mechanics foundations—testing quantum theory and exploring quantum information.
- **Future technologies**: Many proposed quantum technologies rely on entanglement—quantum networks, quantum teleportation, and other applications.

# Scientific Experiments & Demonstrations
- Study Bell test experiments that confirm entanglement involves genuine quantum correlations, not classical hidden variables.
- Learn about photon entanglement experiments, demonstrating how measuring one photon instantly affects its entangled partner.
- Study quantum computing demonstrations, understanding how entanglement enables quantum algorithms and parallel processing.
- Learn about quantum cryptography systems, understanding how entanglement enables secure communication.
- Study thought experiments (like the EPR paradox) that illustrate entanglement's strange properties, helping visualize quantum correlations.

# How do quantum computers work?
- Quantum computers use quantum bits (qubits) that can exist in superposition.

# How does quantum mechanics work?
- Quantum mechanics describes how particles behave at atomic and subatomic scales.

# Definitions
- A **quantum** is the smallest discrete unit of a physical property.
- In quantum mechanics, particles like **neutrinos**, **quarks**, and **subatomic particles** (protons, neutrons) exhibit unique properties.
- An **atom** is the smallest unit of an element that retains the element's properties.
- **Antimatter** consists of antiparticles with opposite charge to normal matter.

# Additional Information
- The study of entanglement also explores its potential roles in biological processes, though this remains an area of active research.
