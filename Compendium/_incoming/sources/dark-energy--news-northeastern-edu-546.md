# What is dark energy?
- Dark energy is one of the greatest cosmic mysteries.
- The universe started rapidly expanding 13.8 billion years ago, but gravity slowed down this expansion.
- In 1998, scientists discovered that the universe began to expand more quickly about 9 billion years after its formation, attributing this to dark energy.
- Dark energy is invisible and acts like another kind of energy in the universe, pushing things apart rather than pulling them together.
- Approximately 68.3% to 70% of the universe is made up of dark energy.

# The Dark Energy Survey
- The Dark Energy Survey was a six-year international collaboration involving over 400 scientists.
- It utilized DECam, a hypersensitive digital camera built by Fermi National Accelerator Laboratory in the U.S., attached to a telescope in Chile operated by the U.S. National Science Foundation.
- Over 758 nights, the survey recorded data from 669 million galaxies across an eighth of the sky.
- The project provided unprecedented scope and detail for studying dark energy.

# Contributions and Findings
- The Dark Energy Survey's final results refine humanity's understanding of dark energy rather than reinventing it.
- The data fit between two models: one where dark energy density is constant, and another where it evolves over time.
- One of the project’s key contributions is a robust reconstruction of the distribution of matter across the universe using advanced methods for measuring gravitational lensing.
- Gravitational lensing helps determine the mass of distant objects by observing how light from background stars is bent by galaxies.

# Legacy and Future
- The lessons learned and methods developed through the Dark Energy Survey are influencing new efforts to study dark energy, including projects like the Ruben Observatory in Chile, the Euclid Satellite, and the Roman Space Telescope.
- The end of the DES marks a transition to the next generation of astronomers and the next phase of humanity’s efforts to chart the stars.

# Quotes from Scientists
- Jonathan Blazek, assistant physics professor at Northeastern University and co-lead on the project’s modeling and analysis team: "We have not answered all these questions. We have some hints... but we don’t have the answers yet."
- Martin Crocce, co-coordinator of the Dark Energy Survey analysis: "This is the only time it has been done in the current generation of dark energy experiments."

# Conclusion
- The Dark Energy Survey has significantly advanced our understanding of dark energy and its role in the universe.
- While many questions remain unanswered, the project has provided valuable insights and methodologies for future research.
