# Classical Physics: The World Before Quantum
- Isaac Newton’s laws described how planets move, how apples fall, and how forces interact.
- In the 19th century, scientists believed that if they knew everything about a system—its position, velocity, and the forces acting upon it—they could predict its future with absolute certainty. This is known as determinism.
- Light was understood as a wave, electricity and magnetism were unified by James Clerk Maxwell’s equations, and the atomic theory of matter was gaining acceptance.

# The Birth of Quantum Theory: Energy Comes in Quanta
- In 1900, German physicist Max Planck proposed that energy is not continuous but comes in tiny packets called quanta. This marked the birth of quantum theory.
- Planck’s formula matched experimental data perfectly and implied that the energy of oscillating atoms could only take on specific, discrete values.
- In 1905, Albert Einstein extended Planck’s idea to light itself, proposing that light could be thought of as particles called photons. This explained the photoelectric effect.

# Wave-Particle Duality: When Particles Behave Like Waves
- In 1924, Louis de Broglie suggested that particles like electrons could behave like waves.
- Experiments showed that electrons fired through a crystal created interference patterns, confirming wave-like behavior.
- The double-slit experiment demonstrated that electrons interfere with themselves and change their behavior based on observation.

# The Uncertainty Principle: Limits to Knowledge
- Werner Heisenberg formulated the Uncertainty Principle in 1927, stating that precise knowledge of a particle’s position and momentum is impossible.
- This principle reflects the wave nature of particles and shows that the quantum world is not deterministic.

# The Schrödinger Equation: The Engine of Quantum Mechanics
- Erwin Schrödinger developed the Schrödinger equation in 1926, governing how quantum systems evolve over time.
- The wavefunction describes a system’s state, and its square gives the probability of finding a particle at any point.

# Quantum Superposition: All States at Once
- A quantum system can exist in multiple states until measured, as illustrated by Schrödinger’s cat thought experiment.
- Superposition is key to quantum computing, where qubits can be in both 0 and 1 states simultaneously.

# Quantum Entanglement: Spooky Action at a Distance
- When two particles are entangled, their states remain linked regardless of distance.
- Experiments confirm entanglement, which is used in quantum teleportation, encryption, and networks.

# Quantum Tunneling: Passing Through the Impossible
- Particles can tunnel through barriers due to wavefunction spreading.
- Tunneling explains natural phenomena like nuclear fusion and powers technologies such as scanning tunneling microscopes.

# The Measurement Problem: What Does It Mean to Observe?
- The measurement problem concerns how a single outcome emerges from quantum superposition upon observation.
- Different interpretations, including Copenhagen, Many-Worlds, and pilot-wave theories, offer various explanations without definitive proof.

# Quantum Mechanics and Modern Technology
- Quantum mechanics underpins lasers, transistors, LEDs, MRI machines, atomic clocks, and computer chips.
- Emerging technologies like quantum computing, cryptography, and sensors rely on quantum principles.

# Quantum Mechanics and the Universe
- Quantum mechanics explains atom formation, star behavior, black hole radiation (Hawking radiation), and merges with relativity in quantum field theory.
- The Standard Model excludes gravity, leaving unification with general relativity as a major challenge.

# Conclusion: The Quantum Frontier
- Quantum mechanics is strange yet beautiful and profoundly accurate, revealing a probabilistic universe governed by mathematical laws.
- Despite its counterintuitive nature, it has been experimentally confirmed and revolutionized technology. Mysteries like wavefunction nature and quantum gravity remain unresolved.
