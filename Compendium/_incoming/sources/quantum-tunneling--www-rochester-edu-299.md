# Quantum Tunneling: URochester Physicist Explains a Nobel Prize–Winning Discovery
- Three scientists received the 2025 Nobel Prize in Physics for demonstrating that quantum effects, specifically quantum tunneling, can appear in larger, visible systems using superconducting circuits.
- The discovery reveals that the quantum world and classical world aren’t as separate as once believed, showing that strange rules governing atoms can apply to macroscopic systems big enough to hold in your hand.
- Machiel Blok, an assistant professor at the University of Rochester, is researching superconducting circuits with qudits, which are quantum computing units that can exist in multiple states at once.
- In these circuits, electrons can “tunnel” through barriers, allowing qudits to occupy two or more states simultaneously, a key property for building quantum computers.
- Quantum tunneling is at the core of Blok’s research, as it allows particles to pass through barriers they seemingly shouldn’t be able to cross according to classical physics.
- The Nobel Committee awarded John Clarke, Michel Devoret, and John Martinis the 2025 Nobel for “the discovery of macroscopic quantum mechanical tunneling and energy quantization in an electric circuit.”
- Quantum tunneling can be explained using the metaphor of a child throwing a ball at a wall; in quantum mechanics, particles can pass through barriers due to their wave-like nature.
- The research conducted by the 2025 Nobel laureates showed that quantum behavior can appear not only in tiny subatomic particles but also in larger objects made of many particles.
- Blok refers to this phenomenon as creating a “big Schrödinger’s cat,” where systems can exist in multiple states at once.
- At URochester, Blok and his team use superconducting circuits to explore fundamental quantum behavior and develop new quantum technologies, including steps toward quantum computers and ultra-secure quantum communication networks.
- The goal of their research is to use quantum effects to make quantum computers, closely related to the Nobel Prize research.
- Beyond its promise for technological advances, the Nobel research highlights the power of curiosity-driven science; the original experiments were conducted in the 1980s with practical applications not initially in mind.
- Machiel Blok believes that curiosity can lead to deep answers about nature and unimaginable possibilities.
- The University of Rochester is helping shape the future of quantum research, from foundational theory to practical impact, with world-renowned faculty, cutting-edge facilities, and a tradition of innovation.

# What is “Macroscopic Quantum Mechanical Tunneling”?
- Macroscopic quantum mechanical tunneling refers to the phenomenon where particles can pass through barriers they seemingly shouldn’t be able to cross according to classical physics.
- The Nobel Committee awarded John Clarke, Michel Devoret, and John Martinis the 2025 Nobel for this discovery.
- This phenomenon was demonstrated using superconducting circuits, which are large enough to be seen by the naked eye yet still exhibit quantum behavior.

# A Nobel Demonstration

- Their work was conducted in the 1980s and was motivated by a desire to understand physics and nature, without initially considering practical applications.
