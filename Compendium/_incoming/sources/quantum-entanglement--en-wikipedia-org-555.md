# Introduction to Quantum Entanglement
- Quantum entanglement is the phenomenon where the quantum state of each particle in a group cannot be described independently of the others, even when separated by large distances.
- This phenomenon highlights the disparity between classical physics and quantum mechanics, as it is a primary feature of quantum mechanics not present in classical mechanics.

# Historical Development
- The concept was first discussed in a 1935 paper by Albert Einstein, Boris Podolsky, and Nathan Rosen, known as the EPR paradox.
- Erwin Schrödinger independently explored this phenomenon around the same time and introduced the term "entanglement."
- In 1946, John Archibald Wheeler suggested studying entangled pairs of gamma-ray photons, which was later experimentally demonstrated by Chien-Shiung Wu and I. Shaknov in 1949.

# Experimental Verification
- Bell's inequality, proposed in 1964, provided a testable upper limit for correlations in local hidden variable theories.
- Experiments by Stuart Freedman and John Clauser in 1972, and later Alain Aspect in 1982, demonstrated violations of Bell's inequality, confirming the non-classical nature of entanglement.

# Applications and Modern Developments
- The concept of entanglement has been leveraged to propose quantum teleportation, first realized experimentally in 1997.
- Anton Zeilinger pioneered work on entanglement swapping and quantum cryptography using entangled photons.
- In 2022, the Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded to Alain Aspect, John F. Clauser, and Anton Zeilinger for their groundbreaking experiments with entangled photons and contributions to quantum information science.

# Meaning and Implications
- Entanglement is a resource that facilitates communication and computation tasks beyond classical capabilities.
- The mathematical definition of entanglement implies that maximal knowledge about the whole system does not guarantee maximal knowledge about its individual parts.
- Correlations from entangled states cannot be replicated by classical probability theory, making entanglement a unique quantum phenomenon.

# Methods of Achieving Entanglement
- Entanglement can be achieved through various interactions, such as parametric down-conversion in photons or with electrons and other particles.
- The process involves creating systems where the quantum state cannot be factored into separate states for each constituent particle.

# Decoherence and Entanglement Breakdown
- Entanglement is broken when particles interact with their environment, leading to decoherence.
- This interaction entangles the particles with the environment, resulting in the loss of entanglement between the original particles.

# Singlet State and EPR Paradox
- The singlet state is a specific entangled state where two particles are anti-correlated in spin measurements along the same axis.
- This state forms the basis for David Bohm's version of the EPR paradox, challenging classical interpretations of quantum mechanics.

# Nonlocality and Relativistic Considerations
- Measurements of entangled particles can be spacelike separated, making it impossible to transmit information between them faster than light.
- Correlations remain consistent across different relativistic reference frames, highlighting the non-local nature of entanglement.

# Failure of Local Hidden Variable Theories
- Local hidden variable theories fail to explain the observed correlations in entangled particle experiments, as demonstrated by violations of Bell's inequality.
- This failure confirms that quantum mechanics is complete and that entanglement represents a fundamentally non-classical phenomenon.

# [Violation of Bell Inequalities and Nonlocality]
- The violation of Bell inequalities is often referred to as quantum nonlocality.
- Werner states, which exhibit entanglement, cannot power the violation of a Bell inequality despite their entanglement.
- The concept of nonlocality in quantum mechanics is controversial, as it does not necessarily imply faster-than-light signals.
- Quantum field theory is considered local because observables in spacelike-separated regions commute.

# [Quantum States and Entanglement]
- A composite system's state can be represented as a tensor product of the individual systems' Hilbert spaces.
- Separable states (product states) can be written as a tensor product of pure states from each subsystem, while entangled states cannot.
- The Bell states are examples of maximally entangled pure states that cannot be separated into pure states of each subsystem.

# [Measurement and Correlation]
- In an entangled state, the results of measurements on the two subsystems (e.g., A and B) are correlated.
- For the entangled state \( |\Psi\rangle = \frac{|01\rangle + |10\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} \), the reduced density matrix for each subsystem is a mixed state.

# [Entanglement as a Resource]
- Entangled states are considered a resource in quantum information theory, enabling transformations that would otherwise be impossible with local operations alone.
- Teleportation allows transferring an unknown quantum state from one location to another using entanglement and classical communication.
- Entanglement swapping enables two parties who have never interacted to share an entangled state.

# [Multipartite Entanglement]
- Multipartite systems can exhibit different types of entanglement, such as the Greenberger–Horne–Zeilinger (GHZ) state and the W state.
- Tracing out one qubit from a GHZ state results in a separable state, while tracing out one qubit from a W state still leaves an entangled state.

# [Classification of Entanglement]
- Quantum states can be classified based on whether they can be converted into each other using local operations and classical communication (LOCC).
- The concept of monogamy applies to entanglement, meaning a single particle cannot be maximally entangled with more than one other particle at a time.

# SLOCC Classes
- Two states are in the same 'SLOCC class' if they can be transformed into each other by local operations with a probability greater than 0.
- For two pure qubits, there are only two SLOCC classes: entangled states (including Bell states and weakly entangled states) and separable states (product states like |00⟩).

# Distillability of States
- A state is called distillable if it can be transformed into at least one pure entangled state through local operations and classical communication.
- Non-distillable states cannot be used to create entanglement between distant parties.

# Entanglement Criteria
- The Peres–Horodecki criterion provides a necessary and sufficient condition for separability in 2-qubit and qubit-qutrit systems.
- For higher-dimensional systems, the problem of determining separability becomes NP-hard.

# Methods of Creating Entanglement
- Spontaneous parametric down-conversion is commonly used to generate entangled photons in polarization.
- Other methods include fiber couplers, quantum dots, and the Hong–Ou–Mandel effect.
- Entanglement swapping allows creating entanglement between systems that never directly interacted.

# Testing for Entanglement
- A density matrix ρ is separable if it can be written as a convex sum of product states: ρ = ∑ j p j ρ j (A) ⊗ ρ j (B).
- The Peres–Horodecki criterion applies to continuous variable systems, with Simon's condition being necessary and sufficient for certain Gaussian states.

# Applications in Quantum Gravity
- Energy entanglement between a system and a clock system within the universe can give observers the experience of time evolution.
- Mark Van Raamsdonk suggested that spacetime arises as an emergent phenomenon from entangled quantum degrees of freedom on the boundary of spacetime.

# Experiments Demonstrating Entanglement
- Bell tests have consistently found that local hidden variables are inconsistent with physical behavior, supporting quantum mechanics.
- In 2017, Yin et al. achieved a new quantum entanglement distance record of 1,203 km using a Micius satellite and ground bases in Lijian and Delingha.

# Entangled States
- Bell states: |Φ±⟩, |Ψ±⟩.
- GHZ state for M > 2 qubits.
- NOON states for bosonic modes.
- Twin Fock states for enhancing precision measurements.

# In 2023 the LHC using techniques from quantum tomography measured entanglement at the highest energy so far
- The experiment was carried out by the ATLAS detector.
- The spin of top-quark pair production was measured.
- The effect was observed with a more than 5σ level of significance.
- The top quark is the heaviest known particle and has a very short lifetime (τ ≈ 10−25 s).
- The top quark decays before undergoing hadronization (~ 10−23 s) and spin decorrelation (~ 10−21 s).
- Spin information was transferred without much loss to the leptonic decay products caught by the detector.
- The spin polarization and correlation of particles were measured and tested for entanglement using concurrence as well as the Peres–Horodecki criterion.
- The effect has been confirmed in the CMS detector.

# Entanglement of macroscopic objects
- In 2020, researchers reported quantum entanglement between the motion of a millimetre-sized mechanical oscillator and a disparate distant spin system of a cloud of atoms.
- Later work complemented this by quantum-entangling two mechanical oscillators.

# Entanglement of quarks and gluons in protons
- Physicists at Brookhaven National Laboratory demonstrated quantum entanglement within protons.
- Quarks and gluons were shown to be interdependent rather than isolated particles.
- Using high-energy electron-proton collisions, maximal entanglement was revealed, reshaping our understanding of proton structure.

# Quantum information science
- The field involves the study of quantum systems for information processing tasks.
