# Introduction to the Double-Slit Experiment
- The double-slit experiment was first performed in 1801 by Thomas Young to demonstrate light's wave-like nature.
- In modern quantum mechanics, it illustrates that light behaves as both a particle and a wave, with duality that cannot be observed simultaneously.

# Historical Context and Einstein-Bohr Debate
- Albert Einstein and Niels Bohr debated the experiment in 1927, with Einstein arguing for simultaneous observation of light's particle and wave nature.
- Bohr countered using the uncertainty principle, showing detection of a photon's path would erase the interference pattern.

# MIT's Idealized Version of the Experiment
- Researchers used individual atoms as slits and weak light beams to ensure each atom scattered at most one photon.
- By preparing atoms in different quantum states, they modified the information obtained about photon paths.

# Experimental Setup and Procedures
- More than 10,000 ultracold atoms were used, cooled to microkelvin temperatures and arranged in a lattice using laser beams.
- The experiment involved shining weak light beams through these atoms and observing how photons scattered off them.

# Results and Findings
- The researchers confirmed quantum theory predictions: increased information about photon paths reduced interference pattern visibility.
- Einstein's assumption was proven incorrect, as detecting atom movement by photons diminished wave interference.

# Implications and Significance
- The experiment demonstrated that the "springs" (atom slits) do not affect photon duality; only atom fuzziness matters.
- It provided unprecedented precision in testing quantum mechanics principles, aligning with theoretical descriptions.
