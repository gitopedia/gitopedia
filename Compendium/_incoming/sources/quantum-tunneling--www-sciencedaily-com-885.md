# Quantum Tunneling Mystery Solved After 100 Years
- Professor Dong Eon Kim from POSTECH's Department of Physics and Max Planck Korea-POSTECH Initiative, along with his research team, has unraveled the mystery of the 'electron tunneling' process for the first time.
- The study was published in the international journal *Physical Review Letters* and is significant as a key to unlocking the long-standing mystery of 'electron tunneling,' which has remained unsolved for over 100 years.
- Quantum tunneling involves electrons passing through energy barriers (walls) that they seemingly cannot surmount with their energy, as if digging a tunnel through them.
- This phenomenon is the principle by which semiconductors, core components of smartphones and computers, operate, and is also essential for nuclear fusion, the process that produces light and energy in the sun.

# Experimental Setup and Discovery
- The research team conducted an experiment using intense laser pulses to induce electron tunneling in atoms.
- The results revealed a surprising phenomenon: electrons do not simply pass through the barrier but collide again with the atomic nucleus inside the tunnel. This process was named 'under-the-barrier recollision' (UBR).
- It was previously believed that electrons could only interact with the nucleus after exiting the tunnel, but this study confirmed that such interaction can occur inside the tunnel.

# Implications and Significance
- During the under-the-barrier recollision, electrons gain energy inside the barrier and collide again with the nucleus, thereby strengthening 'Freeman resonance.'
- This ionization was significantly greater than that observed in previously known ionization processes and was hardly affected by changes in laser intensity.
- The research is significant as it is the first in the world to elucidate the dynamics of electrons during tunneling.
- It is expected to provide an important scientific foundation for more precise control of electron behavior and increased efficiency in advanced technologies such as semiconductors, quantum computers, and ultrafast lasers that rely on tunneling.

# Quotes from Researchers
- Professor Kim Dong Eon stated, "Through this study, we were able to find clues about how electrons behave when they pass through the atomic wall," and added, "Now, we can finally understand tunneling more deeply and control it as we wish."
