# Quantum Entanglement

## Overview
Quantum entanglement is a fundamental phenomenon in quantum physics where particles become interconnected in such a way that the state of one particle instantly influences the state of another, regardless of the distance separating them. This concept is crucial for understanding quantum mechanics and has significant implications for future technologies like quantum computing and communication.

## Key Concepts  

Quantum entanglement is a fundamental phenomenon in quantum mechanics where particles of the same origin remain connected even after separating by large distances. This connection ensures that any event affecting one particle instantaneously influences all others with which it is entangled, as they share a unified quantum state. The concept was first highlighted by Albert Einstein, who referred to it as "spooky action at a distance" in 1935, and later validated through John Stewart Bell's theorem and experimental confirmations.

Entanglement describes a non-classical correlation between two or more quantum systems, characterized by a single wavefunction that cannot be expressed as a product of individual wavefunctions for each system. This interdependence means that measuring one entangled particle immediately determines the state of its counterpart, regardless of distance. For example, if one qubit is measured as |0⟩, the other collapses to |0⟩, and similarly for |1⟩.

The concept of quantum entanglement was first discussed in a 1935 paper by Albert Einstein, Boris Podolsky, and Nathan Rosen, known as the EPR paradox. Erwin Schrödinger independently explored this phenomenon around the same time and introduced the term "entanglement." In 1946, John Archibald Wheeler suggested studying entangled pairs of gamma-ray photons, which was later experimentally demonstrated by Chien-Shiung Wu and I. Shaknov in 1949.

Bell's inequality, proposed in 1964, provided a testable upper limit for correlations in local hidden variable theories. Experiments by Stuart Freedman and John Clauser in 1972, and later Alain Aspect in 1982, demonstrated violations of Bell's inequality, confirming the non-classical nature of entanglement. In 2022, the Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded to Alain Aspect, John F. Clauser, and Anton Zeilinger for their groundbreaking experiments with entangled photons and contributions to quantum information science.

Entanglement is a resource that facilitates communication and computation tasks beyond classical capabilities. It enables quantum parallelism, allowing quantum computers to perform multiple calculations simultaneously by manipulating many qubits in a single operation. This capability underpins advanced protocols like quantum teleportation and error correction. Entanglement is also crucial for quantum error correction, protecting quantum information from decoherence.

The mathematical definition of entanglement implies that maximal knowledge about the whole system does not guarantee maximal knowledge about its individual parts. Correlations from entangled states cannot be replicated by classical probability theory, making entanglement a unique quantum phenomenon. A composite system's state can be represented as a tensor product of the individual systems' Hilbert spaces. Separable states (product states) can be written as a tensor product of pure states from each subsystem, while entangled states cannot.

In an entangled state, the results of measurements on the two subsystems (e.g., A and B) are correlated. For the entangled state \( |\Psi\rangle = \frac{|01\rangle + |10\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} \), the reduced density matrix for each subsystem is a mixed state. The singlet state, a specific entangled state where two particles are anti-correlated in spin measurements along the same axis, forms the basis for David Bohm's version of the EPR paradox, challenging classical interpretations of quantum mechanics.

Measurements of entangled particles can be spacelike separated, making it impossible to transmit information between them faster than light. Correlations remain consistent across different relativistic reference frames, highlighting the non-local nature of entanglement. Werner states, which exhibit entanglement, cannot power the violation of a Bell inequality despite their entanglement.

Entangled states are considered a resource in quantum information theory, enabling transformations that would otherwise be impossible with local operations alone. Teleportation allows transferring an unknown quantum state from one location to another using entanglement and classical communication. Entanglement swapping enables two parties who have never interacted to share an entangled state.

Multipartite systems can exhibit different types of entanglement, such as the Greenberger–Horne–Zeilinger (GHZ) state and the W state. Tracing out one qubit from a GHZ state results in a separable state, while tracing out one qubit from a W state still leaves an entangled state.

Quantum states can be classified based on whether they can be converted into each other using local operations and classical communication (LOCC). The concept of monogamy applies to entanglement, meaning a single particle cannot be maximally entangled with more than one other particle at a time. Two states are in the same 'SLOCC class' if they can be transformed into each other by local operations with a probability greater than 0.

A state is called distillable if it can be transformed into at least one pure entangled state using LOCC. The Peres–Horodecki criterion provides a necessary condition for separability of mixed states in two dimensions, which has been experimentally confirmed in high-energy physics.

Recent experiments have demonstrated the vast potential of quantum entanglement. In 2017, Yin et al. achieved a new quantum entanglement distance record of 1,203 km using a Micius satellite and ground bases in Lijian and Delingha. In 2020, researchers reported quantum entanglement between the motion of a millimetre-sized mechanical oscillator and a disparate distant spin system of a cloud of atoms. Later work complemented this by quantum-entangling two mechanical oscillators.

In 2023, the ATLAS detector at the LHC measured entanglement in top-quark pair production with a more than 5σ level of significance. The experiment revealed that spin information was transferred without much loss to the leptonic decay products caught by the detector, confirming entanglement using concurrence as well as the Peres–Horodecki criterion.

Entanglement has also been observed in quarks and gluons within protons, reshaping our understanding of proton structure. Physicists at Brookhaven National Laboratory demonstrated that quarks and gluons are interdependent rather than isolated particles, with maximal entanglement revealed through high-energy electron-proton collisions.

These experiments highlight the profound implications of quantum entanglement across various scales, from fundamental particle physics to macroscopic systems, confirming its role as a cornerstone of quantum information science.

## History
The concept of entanglement was met with skepticism by Albert Einstein, who proposed local hidden variable theories to explain the phenomenon as an incomplete understanding rather than non-local connections. However, John Bell's 1960s theoretical work laid the foundation for testing these correlations experimentally. The 1970s experiments by John Clauser and others confirmed entanglement's validity, aligning with quantum mechanics predictions.

## Networks of Entanglement
Entanglement isn't limited to pairs; it can extend to hundreds or even millions of particles, forming complex systems known as many-body entangled states. These systems naturally occur in various contexts, including living organisms and materials, behaving as unified entities despite the lack of direct communication between individual particles.

## Relativity Considerations
A common misconception is that entanglement enables faster-than-light communication, which contradicts Einstein's relativity principles. However, experiments demonstrate that while entangled particles exhibit correlations, they do not transmit information faster than light. This distinction ensures relativity remains intact.

## Proving Entanglement
Multiple experimental tests across various measurement angles have validated the reality of quantum entanglement. Renowned physicist John Preskill highlights its importance as a key theme in 21st-century science, supported by evidence from studies like "Collecting Evidence for Quantum Entanglement."

By understanding and harnessing entanglement, scientists aim to develop transformative technologies that leverage these unique quantum properties.
